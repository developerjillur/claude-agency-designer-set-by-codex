# Script and story research, part 1

Three Hindi (Hinglish) reference videos the owner picked as teaching material on viral scripts and on storytelling that keeps people watching. Written 2026-09-26 as input for our script skill, its lint and its judge.

Short tags used in this file:

- **YASH**: "How I Write my Viral Script", Abhimanyu Singh (Yash), 19:48, id `8oNrdEhksyE`. His method is built for one-minute shorts.
- **LEVELS**: "Only 5 words You Need to Make Addictive Videos.", HimanshuG, 13:50, id `UPkzsr8apq8`. Long form.
- **ERCRT**: "Storytelling Science To Hook Viewer Till End | 4 Years in 10 Mins.", HimanshuG, 10:40, id `qSeXh7V3GXI`. Long form.

## Sources and how they were checked

- All three `.txt` transcripts were read in full. The `.json` sound and music logs were used for the sound-design notes.
- About 100 frames were sampled to read on-screen text (level cards, formula cards, proof graphics), because the "5 words" question and two of the templates below exist only on screen.
- **YASH timing is wrong in the transcript.** The transcription model's clock jumped about 40 s near minute marks. Checked against frames and fixed here: lines shown in the `.txt` between 5:57 and 10:03 are 40 s late, and lines from 10:56 to 15:00 are displayed at 11:40 to 18:19, mixed in with the real 15:00+ lines (so the `.txt` order between 15:00 and 18:20 is scrambled). A fresh transcription of 9:05 to 10:12 recovered about 35 s the `.txt` leaves out (the comment-mining rule, A3 below). Every YASH time below is the real video time; `~` marks a corrected one. The same 40 s signature (one line lasting 42 to 47 s) also shows up in four other transcripts of this batch: `q2bOMglypxg`, `hNuAv-42jzY`, `bKb0GZDhyTE`, `pcnrzBwoVUk`.
- Counts such as words per minute were taken from the transcripts. Hindi word counts include many short postpositions, so they do not compare one to one with English or Bangla.
- Spoken lines are paraphrased in English; the only direct quote is the five-word game question. Short on-screen captions (already in English or romanised Hindi) are given as they appear.

## Top findings

1. The most reusable tool is the LEVELS ladder, used as **five questions plus one line pass**: what happened, in what order, how (beats linked by *but* and *therefore*), why it matters to you (stakes and one big question), what changed; then make every sentence land. It works as a revision process and as a judge scale.
2. YASH's real lesson is about delivery. A script read word for word sounds robotic; a **roadmap** (structure, hard facts, one or two examples) told aloud several times sounds human. For us: output a roadmap form and pass a retell test and a read-aloud test.
3. **Link beats with but or therefore, never "and then"** (LEVELS level 3, credited on screen to South Park co-creator Matt Stone). This is countable.
4. **Stakes = root + risk + urgency**, and the story states **one big question** early (LEVELS level 4).
5. **Pain → gap by contrast → relief** is a precise hook template (LEVELS 7:14, on screen).
6. **A story is a change** from one state to another, positive or negative (ERCRT, LEVELS level 6).
7. **Emotion must rise and fall**; a flat line and a constant peak both bore (ERCRT).
8. **Tease the payoff at the start, pay it late**: ERCRT shows its formula blurred in the first minute and reveals it at 68% of runtime; YASH pays off his 47M case in the last 90 s.
9. **Gate the topic before writing**: a trend, a "wow", a repeated audience demand, or a proven angle (YASH).
10. The "science" in both HimanshuG videos (dopamine loops, oxytocin, neural coupling, pareidolia as proof) is pop neuroscience. Keep the craft, drop the claims.
11. Both HimanshuG rewrites invent facts, and one changes its product halfway (a chaat cart becomes a tea stall). Our skill needs a declared nonfiction mode, a fact ledger and a continuity check.

## Video 1: YASH, "How I Write my Viral Script"

### 1. What it teaches, as a method

**Stance: AI does research, never the words [1:53-3:06].**

- Research uses ChatGPT, Gemini and Perplexity fully; scripting uses AI zero percent. His reason: AI cannot bring emotion, humour or your personal tone.
- Analogy [2:26]: driving with Google Maps you turn when told and stop thinking; driving without it you watch, think and ask people. Reading lines someone else wrote makes you passive in the same way, and the camera and the audience notice (you sound robotic) [2:48].
- What works [2:58]: facts and concept clear in your head, then explained in your own structured but raw, natural way.

**Part A. Topic selection [~6:20-10:54].** The topic is the foundation: with the wrong topic no script, camera or mic brings views [~6:24-~6:40].

- **A1. Trend check [~6:44-~7:58].** Follow the five or six biggest creators in your niche and check their uploads morning and evening. When three or four of them cover the same topic, the trend is confirmed; start at once. Low-time version for people with jobs or school: turn on those creators' notifications and treat the same topic from two or three of them as the signal.
- **A2. Wow factor [~7:58-~9:11].** A trend comes every week but rarely runs five or six days. In between, take whatever makes you say "wow" inside, on a screen or in real life. His claim: what wows you wows others most of the time (he says 90%). Example [~8:35-~8:57]: in winter they kept hearing parents say their kids were insisting on rabbit-ear caps, saw kids wearing them, then found celebrities in them; the wow was the direct line from social media to the street. That became the 47M-view short. Rule: grab a wow topic immediately.
- **A3. Comment mining [~9:17-~10:03]** (missing from the `.txt`, recovered). Read what viewers ask for under your earlier videos. Do not make a video for every single comment; act on what the majority asks. This very video came from 100+ such requests. A small channel can mine a big creator's comments instead.
- **A4. Research account [10:03-10:54].** A second YouTube or Instagram account that follows only creators in your niche, so its home feed turns into a niche feed. They keep two or three (one for business topics, for example). Topics and angles appear without searching.

**Part B. Research with AI, checked by hand [10:54-~12:27].**

- **B1.** Ask ChatGPT and Gemini the same things; accept a fact only when both agree, then check it yourself on the web [~11:14-~11:28].
- **B2.** Use Perplexity for per-fact sources, and confirm the source of every fact that will be spoken in the video [~11:28-~11:39].
- **B3.** Always prompt the AI to say where each piece of information comes from, because it states wrong things with confidence [~11:39-~11:56].
- **B4. Angle mining from a hit [~11:56-~12:27].** Give the AI everything about a video that worked (topic, format, the direction of the argument, duration, the comments, sometimes the transcript) and ask for similar topics and ten different angles.
- Recap [~12:30]: trends, wow factor, comments or research account, AI research with manual verification.

**Part C. Scripting: a natural flow, not word for word [~12:54-16:30].**

- **C1. Learn it deeper than needed [~13:00-~13:25].** Before choosing any narrative, learn the topic like a first-time learner; they watch at least five videos on it. For "how did Zepto grow so fast": its whole story, business model, strategies, and a breakdown of its marketing [15:17].
- **C2. Tell it to friends [~13:25-~13:59].** Explain it casually to four or five friends, in the "you know what happened? then... and in the end..." register. Each telling comes out in a different order; the brain settles on a natural sequence, and you are relaxed because they are friends. The flow that survives these tellings is the script.
- **C3. Write a roadmap, not a script [~14:03-~14:53].** Three blocks: (a) the structure: where you start, the points in the middle, where you end; (b) hard facts written separately: numbers, dates, names, because those are what you forget or get wrong on camera, and getting them right is your credibility; (c) the one or two examples or stories you will use.
- **C4. Keep it short [15:01-15:11].** One or two pages at most; extra facts are useless in a one-minute video.
- **C5. Worked roadmap, Zepto [15:27-16:08].** Hook line: the app you order from today was about to shut down in 2021 → point 1: many competitors already existed, so how did it get in? → the difference as a number: average delivery of 30 minutes cut to 10 → a human fact: a 19-year-old Stanford founder → the strategy: dark stores → explain how a dark store works, not just its name → a comparison: the neighbourhood kirana shop against a dark store → scale: valuation from $900M to $1.4B → closing reframe: not just a delivery app, a change in behaviour.
- **C6.** With the facts in hand, the brain frames the rest; tell it to a friend two or three times and you will know where each fact goes [16:09-16:30].

**Part D. Delivery on camera [16:30-18:21].**

- **D1. Rehearse on camera** two or three times with the same roadmap before the real take (they did for this video) [16:39, 17:19]. Why: friends react to each sentence, which creates flow; a lens gives nothing back, so a beginner's brain gets no input [16:50-17:11]. After a few takes the brain accepts the camera as a friend and the flow returns [17:11-17:31].
- **D2.** Speak in your own flow, hands free, as if unscripted; viewers read that as confidence [17:31-17:48].
- **D3. Prop or product hook [17:51-18:18].** If the topic has a physical product, hold it. In the cap short he held the cap; viewers saw something different and relatable and stopped scrolling. It also lifts your own confidence.

**Case recap [18:21-19:10], the rabbit-cap short end to end.** Topic: a real-life wow (kids and parents), then social checks (an actress, Rashmika Mandanna, had promoted the cap). Research: ChatGPT and Gemini cross-check, Perplexity for sources: when the trend started, who started it, which film promotion it came from. Script: no word-for-word reading; a roadmap of *hook line, trend, reason, social media's role, sales impact*. Camera: the same cap in hand, facts from notes, spoken as to a friend.

### 2. Mechanics that can become checks or templates

1. **Topic gate.** The brief names at least one signal: *trend* (three or more of the niche's top five or six creators covered it recently; light version: two or three of the creators you follow); *wow* (one sentence stating a surprising link or contrast, for example "celebrities on Instagram → kids on the street"); *demand* (a request repeated by many comments, never one comment); *proven angle* (one of ten angle variations of our own best video).
2. **Fact ledger.** Every number, date and proper name in the script sits in a separate list with a source link. Surprising claims need two independent sources. An AI answer is never a source.
3. **Roadmap output** for on-camera creators: STRUCTURE (open line, three to six middle points, closing line), FACTS (numbers, dates, names), EXAMPLES (one or two). For a short it fits on one page.
4. **Business-story template** (from Zepto): paradox hook (big and familiar today, nearly dead before) → the entry problem as a question → the difference as a before and after number → one human detail → the mechanism named → the mechanism explained through a comparison the viewer already knows → a scale number → reframe close ("not just X, it's Y").
5. **Trend-explainer template** (from the cap short): hook line → what is trending, as seen on the ground → why → the role of social media or a celebrity → the money or sales impact.
6. **Retell test.** The story can be told to a friend as "you know what happened? ... then ... and in the end ..." with only the facts list in hand. Any line that cannot be said that way gets rewritten.
7. **Concept rule.** Every named concept gets a working explanation plus a comparison with something the audience already uses (dark store against kirana).
8. **Delivery notes.** At least two on-camera rehearsals; the notes hold facts only, not sentences.
9. **Prop rule.** If there is a product, it is in hand or in frame from the first seconds.
10. **The opening this video uses:** three audience questions → demand proof → a promise of their real process rather than theory → a result despite missing resources → a conflict anecdote → the promise → a map of the parts.

### 4. Weak, unproven or questionable advice

1. **Zero percent AI in scripting.** What he actually describes is the harm of reading any script word for word, whoever wrote it. The cure he teaches (roadmap, retelling, rehearsal) works on an AI draft as well. The absolutism also markets the creator against agencies.
2. **The 90% wow claim** (what wows you will wow others). No data. The creator's taste is not the audience's. It needs an outside signal (comments, search interest, what other creators are covering).
3. **Three or four niche creators on a topic** confirms a trend, but by then it is late and crowded. It is a confirmation rule, not a discovery rule.
4. **Two chatbots agreeing is not verification.** They share training data and fail the same way. The manual web check and the source confirmation are the real safeguard.
5. **The one-or-two-page limit for a one-minute video** does not constrain anything: a minute of speech is roughly 150 to 240 Hindi words, well under a page. The limit describes research notes, not the script.
6. **Telling four or five friends** is sound oral drafting but impractical at a daily pace and impossible to verify. We need a machine stand-in (retell and read-aloud checks).
7. **The 47M views** are credited to research, emotion and communication, while a celebrity-driven winter craze, timing and the product in hand are not controlled. One success, no comparison.
8. **The Zepto example gives dramatic facts with no source**, for instance that it was about to shut down in 2021. I could not confirm that line, and the video gives nothing to check it against. The worked example breaks the video's own verification rule, which is exactly what a fact-ledger lint should catch.
9. **The claim that props stop the scroll** is plausible but rests on one short.
10. **The video's own shape** works against its advice: six minutes pass before the first step, and the sponsor comes before any how-to content.

## Video 2: LEVELS, "Only 5 words You Need to Make Addictive Videos."

### About the "5 words"

The audio never lists five words. Two readings fit what is on screen and in the audio:

- **Literal:** the cold-open question is exactly five Hindi words, प्यार करोगे कि लात मारोगे? ("Will you love me or kick me?"), a childhood game the video uses as its metaphor for levels. On screen it appears as a three-word caption.
- **Useful:** the recap card at [13:14-13:32] gives each level one short question: *Kya hua* (what happened?), *Sequence?*, *Kaise* (how?), *Kyon matter karta* (why does it matter?), *Change?*; level 5 is an instruction instead (make every sentence addictive). That is five questions plus one line pass, and it is what this file adopts.

Either way the title's promise is not delivered as stated (see weak points).

### 1. What it teaches, as a method

**Core story**, the raw facts reused at every level [0:37-0:52]: Rohit keeps failing big job interviews, gets desperate, opens a chaat cart, struggles, and his approach turns him into a businessman.

**Level 1: rote parrot, fact reader, reporter [1:11-2:22].**

- Tells what happened, with dates and numbers: failed his final corporate interview in 2024; three months later put 15,000 into a chaat cart; under 200 a day for the first 60 days; in month three switched to a premium regional matki chaat; sales now up 400%.
- Verdict: no soul. Viewers get information but cannot connect, learn or engage. He likens it to a TV detective briefing his boss (a CID joke). News channels use it because it is quick and they have a lot of data; a few creators (he names Sarthak and Unfiltered by Samdish) put emotion even into reporting; most new creators only report, so they never connect.
- On screen: a retention curve labelled "No Hook" sliding below a good-video benchmark.

**Level 2: systematic kid, chronologist [2:27-3:41].**

- Tells events in order with simple causes: he always wanted a steady job, failed, broke down; so he opened a cart; nobody bought; the bigger problem was three rival carts at the same spot; he saw people wanted quality and variety; he launched a special masala chaat; since then he has bought the three rival carts and owns the area.
- Verdict: order and difficulties create some pull, but details are thin, the transitions are linear and predictable, and it sounds like a schoolkid describing their day. Viewers leave midway.

**Level 3: time broken for impact, the but and therefore format [3:44-5:34].**

- Rewrite: open on one moment (a Tuesday, heavy rain, Rohit staring at a rejection letter that means his family could lose the house) → jump six months ahead (he is turning down investors who want to buy his business) → an underdog line (a boy with no business experience being ground down by a city that never stops) → where he is now (he dominates the city) → a loop line: before asking how, we need to know when.
- Principle: level 2 links beats with "so" and "then"; level 3 reorders them and links them with *but* and *therefore*, so the next beat cannot be predicted. The format is credited through a clip labelled Matt Stone (South Park co-creator; the rule is their well-known advice to replace "and then" between beats with "but" or "therefore"). He says marketers use it as a domino effect and YouTube now does too.
- Analogy: the story is light travelling forward; *but* is a mirror that bends it. The viewer expected one thing, got another, and now wants to know what happens next.
- On screen, each beat of the rewrite is tagged "this happened / but this happened / therefore this happened", followed by a domino card that says it opens a curiosity loop.

**Level 4: the psychologist, "empathy engineer" [5:34-7:54].** He calls the tools dopamine loops and gives four:

1. **Raise the stakes**, three ways: connect to the character's roots (who he is, what he carries), put something at risk, add urgency. Rewrite: the choking feeling of failing in public and then facing relatives who always doubted you; Rohit has just two weeks to make a big decision with his home on the line; and what does he do? He opens a chaat cart, with an MBA.
2. **The big question**: which big question does your story raise? (Two weeks, the house at stake, and what did he do? What next? How will he survive?) It widens the curiosity gap.
3. **The fake end**: lead the viewer toward one destination, then turn (what *but* does).
4. **Re-hook again and again**, so tension never drops.

- From level 4 on, visual storytelling carries much of the load (he cites Shivanshu Agrawal and Magnate Media).
- A structure: **pain → curiosity gap → relief.** The on-screen example: pain, you spent 100 hours editing a video and still got no results; gap by contrast, but actually three steps get you quick results with almost no editing; relief, give the steps 1, 2, 3.
- Self-analysis [7:14-7:44]: he replays his own opening and labels it: the promise plus the question that delays it is pain plus gap; everything after is relief; each level works as a loop (what is on the next level? the bar keeps rising), which is how the tension lasts.

**Aside: the human element [7:54-8:37].** Scripts are only structure. Write purely by structure and you lose the human element that levels 5 and 6 need. Talk like a person and stop worrying about what viewers think; without engaging talk, scripting, story and visuals all fall flat. He adds that AI cannot do this.

**Level 5: micro-copywriter, "wordsmith" [8:37-10:40].**

- Knows the eight things people react to: survival, approval, safety, companionship, comfort, status, protection, hunger. Any viral video triggers one of them; if yours does, people are more likely to finish it and praise it.
- Also metaphor, pacing, and "visceral verbs": words chosen for impact (which is why school pushed grammar).
- Rewrite: rejection personified (it screams, it does not whisper); its sound is an envelope tearing; he watches the HR manager laugh at his CV in front of his father; he comes home to cry in a corner, but the home-loan EMIs and medicine bills make him stand up; he sells his shame first and stakes his 15,000 savings on a cart; a kettle in a graduate's hand is social death; he stops begging for approval and turns a 10-rupee glass into an elite habit that office workers visit before work.
- Each image gets its own sound (envelope tear, mocking laughter, sobbing, kettle clink); the list of eight appears line by line with a ping each.

**Level 6: the filmmaker's level [10:56-13:08].**

- Rewrite: start in the viewer's world (open your LinkedIn feed; it teaches that a 9-to-5 cubicle is the ultimate safety net) → call it the biggest lie, one 90% of people miss, as Rohit did → a ten-minute interview did not just leave him broke, it left him stripped bare → a desperate move: a roadside chaat cart → twist: that was his biggest mistake, as he learned when his own relatives mocked him at his cart, asking why, when and how → reversal: he started selling exactly those questions, so people could connect with him → lesson: to gain something you give something up, sometimes your shame → a closing image that calls back to the files kept in those cubicles.
- Explanation: level 6 is about small nuances (a metaphor, a shift in emotion, a turn in the story). A story is not a story without change, in mindset, thoughts or opinions; value sits where something is gained or changes. Plus cultural nuance, which makes it feel real and matters more now that AI writes scripts: AI does not know his culture. For his audience "reporting" means old Doordarshan-style news, so that is the metaphor to use. Level 6 combines all earlier levels.

**Recap [13:14-13:32].** Level 1 tells what happened; 2 the order; 3 how; 4 why it matters to you; 5 makes every sentence addictive; 6 changes how you think and see life. Add one per level.

### 2. Mechanics that can become checks or templates

1. **Revision ladder**, six passes on every draft: (1) facts, what happened; (2) order, in what sequence; (3) how, every beat linked to the next by *but* or *therefore*, and the opening reordered for impact; (4) why it matters: root, risk, urgency, one big question, one fake end, a re-hook at each section; (5) lines: a sensory image, a strong verb, a metaphor only where it helps; (6) change: the belief or behaviour that shifts, plus one culture-specific detail.
2. **Connector rule.** Between beats use *but* (conflict) or *therefore / so* (consequence); "and then" links fail. Countable per language: Hindi लेकिन / मगर / पर and इसलिए / इसीलिए against फिर / उसके बाद; Bangla কিন্তু / তবে and তাই / ফলে / সেজন্য against তারপর / এরপর.
3. **Level-3 cold open:** [day, weather or other sensory setting] + [the character looking at an object that carries the stakes] + [what it means for the people they love] → [a time jump with a number, "six months later"] + [the opposite situation] → [an underdog line] → [where things stand now] → [loop line: before how, when].
4. **Stakes fields:** root (who he is, what he carries), risk (what he can lose, concrete: the house), urgency (a deadline with a number: two weeks).
5. **One big question**, stated in the first 20% of the script and answered late.
6. **At least one fake end** or change of direction.
7. **Re-hook at every section boundary** (a new level, a new question, a higher bar).
8. **Pain → gap → relief:** the pain in the viewer's words with a number (100 hours of editing); the gap as a contrast sentence that starts with "but actually" (three steps, almost no editing); the relief as numbered steps.
9. **Desire tag:** the core beat serves one of the eight desires; record which.
10. **Level-6 opener:** open your [app the viewer uses daily]; it tells you [a common belief]; that is the biggest lie.
11. **Change line:** one sentence with the belief before and after.
12. **Culture token:** at least one reference only the target audience would catch (a TV show, an institution, an everyday money pressure).

### 3. How the video itself is built

- **0:00-0:05, promise with proof:** on screen his average view duration jumps from 0:57 (2024) to 4:57 (2026, last two months), while he promises to move retention from one level to the other just by putting the right words in the right place.
- **0:05-0:15, skit interrupt:** he delays the answer with a question first: the childhood game; a friend picks the kick, a cartoon kick sound, a groan; the big caption PYAAR YA LAAT?
- **0:15-0:32, bridge:** the game has levels, so does storytelling; a joke promise to ruin Rohit's life for the lesson (a running character with comic value).
- **0:32-0:54, the core story in 20 s**, over an illustrated cutout of Rohit.
- **0:54-1:08, tease:** level 6 is what big creators, writers and filmmakers use; a six-part progress bar; then he insists the lesson has to start at level one.
- **Levels start at 1:11, 2:27, 3:44, 5:34, 8:37 and 10:40** (gaps of 76, 77, 110, 183 and 123 s). Each opens with a retro game-style card and a persona name, then the rewrite as on-screen text, a critique, and a pop reference (CID, a schoolkid, Matt Stone, a physics mirror, Doordarshan).
- **Delay device:** three "but before that" deferrals in the first 70 s (0:05, 0:32, 1:07).
- **Meta break [7:14-7:44]:** his own opening replayed in black and white and dissected.
- **Plugs:** an older video (7:49) and a paid hooks guide with a discount code (10:43), both placed before level 6, so viewers stay for the last rung.
- **Recap card [13:14]:** a table of level, question and emoji.
- **Ending:** a pointer to another video for level 7 (an end-screen open loop), his sign-off, a meme outro.
- **Sound:** 24 logged effects, among them rain and thunder under the level-3 rewrite, a boom on the level-4 card, pings for the eight desires, the envelope tear, mocking laughter, sobbing, a kettle clink, applause.
- **Numbers:** 15 question lines (about 1.1 per minute); the longest stretch without a question is 8:16 to 12:48 (the level-5 and level-6 prose plus the plug), where the video sags. *But*-type connectors 26, *therefore*-type 14, *then*-type only 6: the video practises its own rule.
- **Shape:** a ladder. The same story rewritten six times, so the viewer hears the improvement and wants the next rung.

### 4. Weak, unproven or questionable advice

1. **Title and content do not match.** No five words are taught; the number is curiosity bait.
2. **Level 3 mixes two separate tools:** reordering time (a flash-forward, starting in the middle) and linking beats by cause or conflict (*but / therefore*). His own wording of the difference between levels 2 and 3 is muddled ("therefore" appears in both). Our rules keep them as separate checks.
3. **"Dopamine loops"** is a label, not evidence. The four tools are ordinary craft.
4. **The eight desires** match the "Life Force 8" list from Drew Eric Whitman's copywriting book *Cashvertising*, uncredited. It is a copywriting heuristic; the claim that every viral video triggers one cannot fail, because the categories are that broad.
5. **The rewrites invent facts** (an MBA, a two-week deadline, the HR manager laughing in front of his father, investors, relatives at the cart). Fine in fiction, fabrication in nonfiction. Our skill needs a declared mode.
6. **Continuity slip:** in the level-5 rewrite the chaat cart turns into a kettle and a 10-rupee glass, that is, a tea stall. A judge must catch drift in product, place and numbers.
7. **Purple prose:** a kettle as a social death sentence, office workers as corporate slaves, an interview that stripped him bare. It reads as melodrama and insults office workers, who are a large part of the audience. Level 5 done badly is worse than level 2.
8. **The level-6 payoff dodges.** The rewrite says he began selling those very questions, then tells the viewer they already know what happened next; the viewer never learns what he actually did. A vague payoff should fail.
9. **The claim that AI cannot supply the human element or cultural nuance** is overstated. The gap is missing context, which we can supply (the creator's own stories, local references); still, a generic prompt does give a generic script.
10. **The 0:57 → 4:57 retention jump** proves nothing about the levels; video length, topic and audience growth all move average view duration.

## Video 3: ERCRT, "Storytelling Science To Hook Viewer Till End"

### 1. What it teaches, as a method

1. **Brains hunt for stories (he calls it pareidolia) [1:40-2:45].** We see faces in clouds and in the fronts of cars. Demo: two triangles and a circle moving; viewers invent stories (two boys fighting over a girl, two brothers and a toy). Conclusion: put stories in your content so people can relate. Comment prompt: write the story you saw.
2. **A good story is change [2:53-4:15].** Who the character was against who they became (Breaking Bad: a mild teacher becomes a drug kingpin); the journey between is the story. His opening channel graph is this video's own story: a hard-working beginner stuck on a flat line, then growth. Pick a direction: positive, or negative for horror, dark, failure and analysis videos (millionaire to pauper) so people learn from it. In content the change can also be emotional: make them laugh, then make them cry by the end.
3. **Neural coupling [4:46-6:11].** When viewers take in your story, their brains sync with it and they picture it. Demo: an audio-drama style passage about a man running, a heart attack about to strike, remembering the medicine he still has to take home, with panting and heartbeat sounds; then the old "don't think of an elephant" trick and a line about a man on a motorbike (you picture both at once). Application: the line in his cold open about believing he had cracked the algorithm targets beginner creators, so they recognise themselves.
4. **Oxytocin [6:11-7:04].** The bonding hormone keeps viewers attached. Content is king, emotion is queen. Hundred-day challenge series bond most, because viewers live the journey for 100 days (clips: fitness creator Ankit Baiyanpuria marking day 100 of his challenge, Monkey Magic's 100 days, a teacher who teaches chemistry through stories).
5. **Formula 1, ERCRT [7:12-9:38]**, revealed as a bonus (two formulas, not one):
   - **E, emotion cycle:** how much emotion rises and falls decides how long people watch. Make them laugh, turn serious, then add spice. A flat line bores and so does a line that only climbs; trailers are the zigzag exception. Game of Thrones: all is well, then every season a spike when a main character dies.
   - **R, relatability:** practical examples from the viewer's own life are a must. He used his own beginner mistake (no script, just rambling).
   - **C, climax at start:** hint early at the payoff and at what you are holding them for. His hint: a line saying that from then on he used one formula in every video, with the formula shown blurred. He calls it childish but says it sometimes works.
   - **R, reason of watching:** what reason do you give? The stated reason was the formula; the real one was the journey from A to B, and the formula was its by-product.
   - **T, twisting patterns:** twists such as the sudden mention of a formula in the cold open, and his admission halfway through that everything so far is incomplete.
6. **Formula 2, WHW: what, how, why [9:38-10:10].** Companies use it for brand building. A story that covers all three is a good story. Applied to this video: why (to show what good storytelling is), how (through his own journey), what (the formula he learned).
7. **Caveat [10:10-10:31].** Storytelling needs editing's spice before it works, and then thumbnail science: story, editing and thumbnail make a triangle (pointing to two of his other videos).

### 2. Mechanics that can become checks or templates

1. **Change line with direction:** from → to, positive or negative, written before drafting.
2. **Emotion map:** tag each beat (funny, serious, tense, sad, relief, surprise). Long form: at least three different tags in any three minutes, no more than two neighbouring beats with the same tag, and one calm-then-spike moment. Shorts: at least one turn.
3. **Relatability:** at least one example taken from the viewer's own situation, ideally the creator's own mistake told in first person.
4. **Climax at start:** within the first 30 to 60 s, name the payoff or show it hidden (blurred, covered, "I'll show you"); reveal it in the last third.
5. **Reason to watch:** one sentence in the first 30 s saying what the viewer gets; the story itself should also be a reason (a journey with an outcome).
6. **Twist count:** at least one turn in a short, two in long form.
7. **WHW check:** the script answers what, how and why.
8. **Imagery test:** at least one passage built on physical, sensory action (running, a heartbeat, medicine) that makes the listener picture a scene.
9. **Serial option:** a journey or challenge series (day X of 100) when the goal is a lasting bond.
10. **Engagement prompts tied to the content** (write the story you saw; tell me if you pictured it), placed mid-video rather than only at the end.

### 3. How the video itself is built

- **0:00-0:56, cold open** over his real YouTube Studio graph from 16 Feb 2020 (flat at zero, captioned "we decided to start"); the years in huge type (2020, 2021, 2022) with glitch sounds. He narrates his mistakes: chasing views blindly, some editing effort, thinking he had cracked the algorithm, filming with no script (a rambling meme). Then the turning-point date (22 December, the year partly lost in the audio), and the line that from then on he used one formula in every video, with the formula shown blurred in a notebook: the climax-at-start device, shown rather than told. A car-to-airport-to-flight metaphor with engine and jet sounds; a bumpy ride, which he calls proof of being alive.
- **0:56-1:36, thesis:** storytelling is the Sachin Tendulkar of content; in 2024 it is the one skill for any career. Clips of a product-launch reveal and a workplace web series, then the hook question: storytelling can hack this generation's brain.
- **1:40-2:45:** the concept plus an interactive demo (triangles, comment ask).
- **2:45:** two promises: what a good story is, and how influencers use it.
- **2:53-4:15:** story as change (Breaking Bad, a callback to the opening graph). At 3:31 an explicit meta line: you are still watching because he has not told you the climax, though he hinted at it.
- **4:15, twist:** all of this is incomplete; his swings from sky to floor; consistency needed a formula (still hidden).

### 4. Weak, unproven or questionable advice

1. **Pareidolia is misapplied.** Faces in clouds is pareidolia; inventing a story for moving shapes is the Heider and Simmel effect (people assign intentions to motion). Both show pattern-seeking; neither proves you must use stories.
2. **Neural coupling is garbled.** The research (Hasson's lab at Princeton, 2010) found a listener's brain activity tracks the speaker's and predicts understanding. His claims that it starts when you blink, that a viewer will then never forget you, and that it can activate the brain's smell regions are not findings of that work (the smell point comes from separate studies of smell-related words).
3. **Oxytocin claims** (hundred-day series release the most; emotional spikes release more) are unmeasured, and research linking stories to oxytocin is disputed and hard to replicate. Metaphor at most.
4. **ERCRT is an ad hoc acronym.** E and T are vague, C is called childish by its own author, nothing is defined, and nothing is tested beyond one channel's graph.
5. **WHW is Simon Sinek's Golden Circle** (why, how, what) renamed, reordered and uncredited; Sinek's whole point is to start with why.
6. **Survivorship.** The channel grew after the formula, but Shorts, niche, upload frequency and the editing effort he himself mentions are never separated.
7. **The claim that storytelling cannot work without editing** contradicts his own audio-podcast example, and doubles as a funnel to his other videos.
8. **Laugh first and make them cry by the end**, and **steep emotion lines belong only in trailers**, are fair heuristics, not rules.
9. **Small factual slip:** Breaking Bad was a cable TV series, not a web series.
10. **The promise that two brain facts make other storytelling videos unnecessary** overpromises; the video delivers two definitions and two acronyms.

## Synthesis for our skills

### A. Where the three agree

1. Facts alone do not hold attention (LEVELS level 1; YASH's "research plus emotion plus communication").
2. A story is a change (LEVELS level 6; ERCRT).
3. Promise the payoff early and pay it late (ERCRT's climax at start; the LEVELS ladder; YASH's 47M case paid at the end).
4. Relatability comes from the viewer's own life and culture (ERCRT's R; LEVELS level 6; YASH's wow spotted on the street).
5. Sound human: talk to a friend, never read aloud (YASH; the LEVELS aside).
6. Generic AI text fails for lack of specific facts, personal experience and culture (all three). For us that is the design brief: feed those in and test for them.
7. Length decides the toolkit. YASH's roadmap is built for one-minute shorts (one hook, a handful of hard facts, one reframe close). LEVELS and ERCRT are long-form devices (a ladder of sections, repeated loops, a delayed reveal). The skill should choose devices by target length, not apply all of them everywhere.

### B. Rules the script skill should adopt

**Before writing, the brief must hold:**

1. A topic signal (trend, wow, demand, proven angle) and a one-line wow: the surprising link or contrast.
2. A fact ledger with sources, and a declared mode: nonfiction or fiction.
3. The change line: from → to, with direction.
4. The viewer's reward (what they get) and the one big question.
5. For story scripts: root, risk, urgency.
6. The target desire (one of the eight) and the planned emotion for each section.
7. Two or three culture tokens for the named audience (country, city or small town, age).
8. For every mechanism: a comparison the audience already knows.

**While writing:**

9. The first line is a hook from the menu below: no greeting, no topic announcement.
10. The payoff is promised or teased in the first 15 s of long form, the first 3 s of a short.
11. Beats are linked by *but* or *therefore*; no "and then" chains.
12. One big question early; a re-hook at every section; at least one turn.
13. Emotion alternates; no long flat stretch and no constant peak.
14. Concrete beats abstract: numbers, names, objects, money amounts the viewer recognises.
15. Line pass: a sensory image per story beat, active verbs, at most one metaphor per section, no melodrama.
16. The close states the change or a reframe ("not just X, it's Y"), then one call to action. No vague payoff.
17. A cue column: a visual or sound cue for each beat (LEVELS pairs almost every image with a sound, about 1.7 cues a minute plus on-screen text).

**Output:**

18. Two forms: a roadmap for on-camera creators (hook line, structure, facts, examples, close line) and a read-aloud script for voiceover or TTS.
19. Both must pass a retell test and a read-aloud test.

**Honesty:**

20. Nonfiction adds no detail beyond the ledger; invented examples are marked as hypothetical.
21. No pop neuroscience stated as fact (dopamine, oxytocin, "neural coupling", "studies show").

### D. Templates

- **T1. Revision ladder** (six passes): see LEVELS mechanics 1.
- **T2. Pain → gap → relief:** "[Pain in the viewer's words, with a number]. But actually [small number] [steps or changes] are enough, with almost no [the pain]. Here they are: 1, 2, 3."
- **T3. Business paradox story:** "[Brand the viewer uses] is huge today. But in [year] it nearly [failed]. [Rivals] already owned the market, so how did it get in? It changed [metric] from [before] to [after]. Behind it: [one human detail]. The trick: [mechanism]. Think of it like [familiar thing] against [the new thing]. Result: [scale number]. So it's not just [X], it's [Y]."
- **T4. Trend explainer:** "[What people are doing, as seen on the street]. It started with [who, when, where]. It spread because [reason]. [Social media or celebrity role]. And the money: [sales or impact number]."
- **T5. Journey with a hidden formula:** journey or graph of the struggle → the relatable mistakes → the turning point (a date) → the formula shown but hidden → lessons that set it up → twist ("all of this is incomplete") → reveal (plus a bonus) → apply it back to the journey → next step.
- **T6. Ladder video:** one raw story told at rising levels, one rung per section, each rung announced as a loop ("the next level is harder"), a recap card at the end.
- **T7. Roadmap card** (YASH): HOOK LINE · STRUCTURE (open, three to six points, close) · FACTS (numbers, dates, names, with sources) · EXAMPLES (one or two) · CLOSE LINE.

### F. What the judge should score

Give the judge only the script, the brief and the fact ledger, never our working notes, so it judges what a viewer would get.

| # | Dimension | 1 | 3 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| J1 | Hook | Greeting or topic announcement | Clear topic and promise | A specific promise with proof or tension, and a question or tease inside the first seconds |
| J2 | Story level (also report the LEVELS rung, 1 to 6, the highest one fully reached) | Rung 1 or 2: facts reported, or a plain sequence | Rung 3: beats turn on *but / therefore*, or a strong non-linear open | Rung 4 to 6: stakes, a big question and a turn (4); plus line craft without melodrama (5); plus a real change in belief with a culture-specific detail (6) |
| J3 | Change clarity | No visible change | Change implied | The judge can write the from → to in one line |
| J4 | Loops paid | Teases left open or oversold | Most paid | Every tease paid, payoff at least as big as the tease, title promise met |
| J5 | Emotion rhythm | One flat tone, or shouting throughout | Some variation | Planned rises and falls, no flat middle |
| J6 | Clarity | Cannot re-explain after one read | Mostly | The judge can re-explain the mechanism using the script's own comparison |
| J7 | Voice | Reads like an essay or a translation | Mostly natural | One person talking to a friend in the target language; passes read-aloud |
| J8 | Truth | Invented specifics or pop science | Minor unsourced claims | Every fact matches the ledger |
| J9 | Cultural fit | Wrong country, currency, references or greeting | Neutral | References, money and numbers land for the named audience |
| J10 | Ending | Trails off, or several CTAs | A plain summary | A reframe or takeaway, one CTA, ends soon after the payoff |

**Hard fails regardless of score:** invented facts in nonfiction, an unpaid title promise, a continuity error, an em dash.

**Two machine judges taken from YASH:**

- *Retell judge:* a model retells the script "to a friend" in five sentences. If beats drop out or change order, the structure is unclear.
- *First-time-learner judge:* a model with no context explains the mechanism after a single read. If it cannot, the explanation or the comparison is missing.

### G. What matters for South Asian audiences (India and Bangladesh)

1. **Register.** Code-mixed everyday speech is the norm: all three keep English technical words (research, flow, retention, structure) inside the Hindi. For Bangladesh use everyday cholito Bangla with the English words people really say; no shuddh Hindi, no sadhu Bangla.
2. **Numbers and money.** Lakh and crore first: YASH says 47 million and then 4.5 crore for the same figure, and the second is the one that lands with this audience. Rupees for India, taka for Bangladesh. Small concrete sums (a 15,000 investment, a 10-rupee glass) feel real; for Bangladesh use Bangladeshi price points, not converted rupees.
3. **The desires that pull hardest here:** approval (relatives, "what will people say"), protection (parents, medicine bills, loan EMIs), safety and status (a secure job against a small business; in Bangladesh the government-job and BCS dream plays the role the corporate job plays in LEVELS), survival (exams, failure), hunger (street food: chaat in India; fuchka, chotpoti and the tea stall in Bangladesh).
4. **Culture tokens must be country-specific:** Doordarshan ↔ BTV; kirana ↔ mudi dokan; Indian film stars ↔ Bangladeshi ones. Cricket works in both, but check current feeling before naming a player: some Bangladeshi cricketers became politically charged after 2024. CID is widely known in both countries.
5. **Platforms.** India: YouTube and Instagram Reels (TikTok has been banned there since 2020). Bangladesh: Facebook first, then YouTube and TikTok. YASH's trend-watching must include Facebook pages and Reels for Bangladeshi clients.
6. **Greetings and sign-offs are religion-coded** (namaskar, "Ram Ram", salam). Stay neutral or match the creator.
7. **Meme and film clips** (CID, film scenes) land in both countries but are copyrighted. Use original skits or licensed sounds.
8. **Family and money stakes land, but do not mock the audience** (the level-5 rewrite that calls office workers slaves insults many viewers), and keep clear of caste, religion and region jokes.
9. **Take the audience from the brief** (the client's market), not from the writer's defaults: the same global-by-default rule we use for images.

### H. What not to adopt

- The absolutist "no AI in scripts" position. We are the AI; the fix is grounding (facts, personal stories, culture) plus retell and read-aloud checks.
- Pop neuroscience as content or as justification.
- Number titles the body does not deliver.
- Invented detail in nonfiction.
- Purple prose as the default meaning of "level 5".
- A sponsor or plug before the first payoff.
