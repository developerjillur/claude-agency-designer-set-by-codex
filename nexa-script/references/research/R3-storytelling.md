# R3: Storytelling for video scripts, talks, ads and articles

Research date: 2026-09-26. Knowledge base for the script-writing skill: why people pay attention, feel, remember and keep watching; the structures professionals use; how to find and test a story; what our judge should check.

Evidence tags:

- **[Strong]** meta-analysis or repeated findings.
- **[Moderate]** good studies, but few, small or not yet replicated.
- **[Mixed]** studies disagree.
- **[Contested]** failed replications or measurement problems.
- **[Craft]** practitioner framework, judged by use rather than experiment.
- **[Book]** taken from a book not re-read in this session.
- **[Unverified]** could not be checked here (the web-search budget ran out partway; some creator claims rest on titles, chapter lists and one transcript segment).

Sources are numbered in section 9 ([S1] to [S64], books [B1] to [B3]).

## 1. Ten findings in one screen

1. Stories persuade by absorbing people ("transportation"); the effect is real but small (r about .17 to .23), so never break absorption. [S1-S3]
2. Curiosity is the best-supported attention engine: a visible, specific gap that narrows over time. It also improves memory for surprising answers. [S4-S6]
3. The Zeigarnik memory claim fails replication; the urge to finish holds (67%). Close every loop. [S7]
4. Suspense (uncertainty about the next beat) and surprise (how far belief just moved) are separate dials. [S8]
5. People remember emotional, engaging moments; put the point at the peak. [S11, S12]
6. Oxytocin and "dopamine hit" claims are pop science; keep the lesson (tension plus a person to care about). [S14-S20]
7. One specific person, place and number beats statistics, modestly; keep numbers out of the emotional peak. [S21-S23]
8. The major frameworks share one engine: a want, a gap between expectation and result, a change, chained by "but" and "therefore", never "and then". [S38, S39, S42]
9. Structure is a choice: six core emotional arcs dominate a corpus of 1,327 novels, and conflict-free kishotenketsu also works. [S36, S40]
10. AI stories are flatter, more positive, more alike, full of cliches and tidy morals; the judge must check for this. [S60-S64]

## 2. The science: what holds up and what does not

### 2.1 Narrative transportation [Strong, small effect]

Green and Brock defined transportation as absorption into a story: attention, imagery and feeling pulled into the story world. More transported readers held more story-consistent beliefs, judged protagonists more warmly and found fewer "false notes"; reducing transportation reduced belief change [S1]. A meta-analysis of 132 effect sizes from 76 papers modelled its causes and consequences [S2]. Braddock and Dillard's meta-analysis found narratives move beliefs (r = .17), attitudes (.19), intentions (.17) and behaviour (.23, only five studies) [S3].

For scripts: the lever is absorption. A false detail, a tonal slip or a sponsor read at the climax pulls viewers out and switches counter-arguing back on. Effects are small, so story strengthens a good argument; it never replaces one.

### 2.2 Curiosity and the information gap [Strong for behaviour, Moderate for the brain mechanism]

Loewenstein describes curiosity as a feeling of deprivation that appears when attention lands on a specific gap between what we know and what we want to know [S4, p. 87]. His triggers read like a hook menu (p. 91): a question or puzzle; a sequence of events with an expected but unknown ending, stronger when the audience makes its own prediction; a violated expectation; knowing someone else has the answer; something once known and forgotten. Curiosity tends to rise as knowledge grows, because attention shifts to the missing piece (p. 89), and it collapses once people feel sure they know the answer (p. 90). Guessing plus feedback raises it (pp. 91-92). Satisfaction often disappoints because relief is instant (p. 92).

In a trivia study, curiosity tracked activity in regions linked to anticipated reward, and higher curiosity predicted better recall of surprising answers one to two weeks later [S5]. High curiosity also improved memory for incidental material shown during the wait, with midbrain and nucleus accumbens activity [S6]. The popular "prediction error" framing (the brain as a prediction machine rewarded by resolved uncertainty) is a fair summary of this cluster, but its primary neuroscience was not checked here [Unverified].

For scripts: give just enough context for the gap to be visible, invite a guess, hold the answer for a beat, pay off with more than the bare fact. A gap nobody can see is not a hook.

### 2.3 Open loops and the Zeigarnik myth [Contested memory claim, Moderate resumption effect]

A 2025 meta-analysis of 59 publications found no reliable recall advantage for interrupted over finished tasks (weighted ratio 0.99). People did resume interrupted tasks about 67% of the time: the Ovsiankina effect [S7].

For scripts: open loops work through curiosity and the urge to finish, not memory. An unclosed loop is a broken promise, and broken promises teach viewers to ignore the next one.

### 2.4 Suspense, surprise, reversal, tension and release [Moderate]

Ely, Frankel and Kamenica give usable definitions: a moment has more suspense when the next beat could move the audience's beliefs a lot, and more surprise when belief has just moved far from where it was [S8]. Suspense lives before a reveal; surprise lives in it. Aristotle ranked reversal (action turning to its opposite) and recognition (ignorance becoming knowledge) as the strongest plot moments, best when they coincide [S29, Part XI]. Hitchcock's bomb-under-the-table example makes the same split: tell the audience about the bomb and you trade seconds of surprise for minutes of suspense [B1]. Duarte describes good talks as tension and release in waves [S48].

Spoilers are [Mixed]: one set of experiments with 12 short stories found spoiled versions enjoyed more [S9]; later studies found spoilers reduced enjoyment and suspense [S10].

For scripts: suspense does not need a hidden outcome; it needs a viewer who cares and does not know how or when. Mystery (hide the fact) and suspense (show the danger) are separate tools. Tension that is never released tires people; release small loops along the way.

### 2.5 Emotion, engagement and memory [Strong for arousal and memory, Moderate for neural engagement]

Cahill and McGaugh showed identical slides with two narrations; the emotionally arousing version produced a stronger reaction and better memory two weeks later [S11]. Engagement rises and falls similarly across viewers of a TV episode or an audio story, peaks at emotional moments, and predicts which events people later recall [S12]. In a speaker-listener fMRI study, listeners' brain activity tracked the speaker's, the coupling vanished when communication failed, and more anticipatory coupling went with better comprehension [S13].

For scripts: place the point that must be remembered inside or right after an emotional peak. Because engagement is shared, one careful reader's tension map says a lot about an audience.

### 2.6 Paul Zak, oxytocin and "dopamine" [Contested]

Zak's HBR article says blood draws showed character-driven stories trigger oxytocin, that the amount predicted donations, and that a story must first build tension to hold attention [S14]. A related study found heart-related physiology during a short film about a father and his terminally ill son predicted giving [S15]. The hormone story is shaky: a critical review found oxytocin-trust evidence not robust [S16]; common blood assays for oxytocin lacked validation [S17]; intranasal studies were underpowered [S18]; a large registered replication found no effect of oxytocin on trust in its main test [S19]; Ed Yong summarised these problems [S20]. Zak is also CEO of Immersion Neuroscience, which sells engagement measurement [S14, author note].

"Dopamine hits" stretch the curiosity findings in 2.2: curiosity engages reward-anticipation circuits and helps memory [S5, S6]; nothing shows each twist delivers a dose.

For scripts: keep the behavioural lesson (tension plus a person to care about moves people). Never claim hormones in copy.

### 2.7 Specific beats abstract [Strong for concreteness, Moderate for identifiable victims]

Concreteness went with higher comprehensibility, interest and recall across four text types [S21]. In a donation study, a letter about one named girl drew $2.38 on average against $1.14 for statistics about the wider crisis; adding the statistics to her story cut giving to $1.43 [S22]. Across 41 studies the identifiable victim effect is small (r = .05) and reliable mainly for a single identified person [S23]. Experts drift into abstraction because they forget what not knowing feels like, the "curse of knowledge" [S24]; the tapping study behind that idea (tappers expected listeners to name about half the songs; listeners named about 1 in 40) is reported in Made to Stick [B2].

For scripts: one person, one place, one object, one number per idea. Keep statistics out of the emotional moment; bring them in afterwards as proof.

### 2.8 Character and identification [Moderate science, strong craft consensus]

Identification means imaginatively taking on a character's goals and point of view [S25]. People even read goals and chases into moving triangles [S26]. Will Storr argues the brain keeps asking what caused this and what happens next, and that characters come alive through a precisely stated flaw (his example: someone who always thinks she is the only adult in the room) which the plot then tests [S43]. Lisa Cron defines story as how events affect someone pursuing a difficult goal and how that person changes; early on, the brain wants to know whose story it is, what is happening and what is at stake [S44]. Storr and Cron are writers' syntheses: useful craft, not primary evidence.

For scripts: every piece needs a "who" with a want you can say in one line. In factual work the character can be a researcher, a customer, a town or an object, but it must want something and meet resistance.

### 2.9 Pop-science claims to keep out of scripts and pitches

| Claim | Status | Say instead |
|---|---|---|
| "Stories are 22 times more memorable than facts" | No traceable study; the Bruner attribution has no citation [S27] | "People often recall stories better; the size varies" |
| "Attention span is 8 seconds, less than a goldfish" | From a 2015 Microsoft Canada report that borrowed the figure from a statistics website; no underlying research [S28] | People drop irrelevant content fast and binge relevant content |
| "Stories release oxytocin, the moral molecule" | Contested [S16-S20] | Tension plus a person to care about predicts giving in some studies |
| "Every twist is a dopamine hit" | Oversimplified [S5, S6] | Curiosity engages reward anticipation and helps memory |
| "Open loops stick in memory" | Not reliable [S7] | People want to finish what was started |
| "Every good story is the hero's journey" | Comparative-myth thesis criticised for selective sampling [S31]; other shapes work [S36, S40] | Choose structure by format and goal |
| "Stories sync our brains" | Small fMRI findings stretched into a slogan [S13] | Listeners track and anticipate a clear speaker |

## 3. Structures toolkit mapped to script beats

| Structure | Beats | Engine | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Three acts [S29, S30] | Setup ending in an inciting incident and dramatic question; confrontation with rising trouble; climax that answers it | Aristotle's whole: beginning, middle, end joined by necessity; popularised for film by Syd Field (1979) | Anything over 2 minutes |
| Hero's journey [S31] | Departure, initiation, return (17 stages in Campbell 1949; 12 in Vogler's 1985 memo) | Transformation through ordeal | Founder and transformation stories; too heavy for shorts; critics say Campbell chose stories that fit |
| Story Circle, Dan Harmon [S32] | You, need, go, search, find, take, return, change | A lean hero's journey: order into chaos and back, changed | Episodes, series, tutorials framed as journeys |
| Story Spine, Kenn Adams [S33, S34] | Once upon a time / every day / but one day / because of that (x3) / until finally / ever since then | Routine, break, chain of consequences | Fast drafting and causality checks; created around 1991 for improv, brought to Pixar around 1997, spread by Emma Coats's 2011 rules |
| Save the Cat, Blake Snyder [S35] | 15 beats; in a 110-page script: catalyst p. 12, break into two p. 25, midpoint p. 55, all is lost p. 75, break into three p. 85 | Proportion; midpoint raises stakes; low point before the finale | Long-form: convert pages to % of runtime |
| Kishotenketsu [S36] | Introduction, development, twist, reconciliation | A shift of perspective instead of a fight; from four-line Chinese poetry | Brand films, slice of life, "look again" shorts |
| In medias res [S37] | Start at the most charged moment, backfill later | Horace praised Homer for rushing into the middle instead of starting from the egg | Cold opens and hooks |
| Nested loops [Craft] | Open story A, open B, deliver core C, close B, close A | Each open story carries attention through the next | Talks, long videos; popularised by presentation blogs, no primary source |
| ABT, Randy Olson [S38, S39] | And (setup), But (problem), Therefore (action) | Replace "and then" with "but" or "therefore" (Frank Daniel 1986; Parker and Stone 2011; Olson 2013 and 2015) | Loglines, hooks, abstracts, ads |
| Vonnegut's shapes [S40, S41] | Good or ill fortune plotted over time | Six core arcs dominate 1,327 Project Gutenberg novels: rags to riches, riches to rags, man in a hole, Icarus, Cinderella, Oedipus | Planning the emotional line |
| McKee's gap [S42] | Action, expected result, different result, new action | The distance between expectation and result drives each scene | Scene-level test |
| Storr's sacred flaw [S43] | A flawed theory of control is tested, broken, rebuilt | Change of self | Profiles, founder stories |
| Cron's inner issue [S44] | External goal against an inner misbelief | Plot forces the inner change | Character-led ads and documentaries |
| Dicks, Storyworthy [S45] | Find the five-second moment of change; start at its opposite | Change is the story | Personal stories, talks |
| Kindra Hall [S46] | Normal, explosion, new normal | Identifiable characters, authentic emotion, a significant moment, specific details | Value, founder, purpose and customer stories |
| StoryBrand SB7 [S47] | Customer with a problem meets a guide with a plan, is called to act, avoids failure, reaches success | Customer is the hero, brand is the guide | Landing pages, ads, sales videos |
| Duarte's sparkline [S48] | Alternate "what is" and "what could be"; end on the "new bliss"; plan one STAR moment [Book] | Contrast in waves | Keynotes, pitches |
| Heaths' SUCCESs [S49, B2] | Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories | Break a schema, anchor in concrete detail | Checklist for any idea |

On shapes: the most-downloaded arcs were Icarus (rise, fall), Oedipus (fall, rise, fall) and two man-in-a-hole arcs back to back, with downloads admitted to be a rough proxy [S40]. Vonnegut's thesis on story shapes was rejected by the University of Chicago; he received the degree in 1971 for Cat's Cradle [S41].

### 3.1 Beat maps by format [Craft, our synthesis]

**T1 Short-form, 30 to 60 s.** (1) 0-2 s: hook as contrast or question, ideally the most surprising moment shown first. (2) 2-8 s: only the context needed to see the gap. (3) BUT: first complication. (4) SO: what it forces. (5) One or two more BUT and SO turns, 5 to 10 s each. (6) Payoff that answers the hook exactly. (7) Last 1-2 s: callback or re-hook that closes the loop.

**T2 Explainer, 6 to 15 min** (percent of runtime). 0-5%: cold open in medias res, the question in one sentence, the stakes. 5-20%: the normal world or common belief, then the anomaly. 20-50%: investigation with two or three BUT turns, each section ending on a re-hook. About 50%: reversal that reframes the question. 50-75%: complications, the best counter-argument, the cost. About 75%: low point, the favourite explanation fails or the stakes land. 75-95%: the answer, then what it means for the viewer. Last shot: callback to the cold open.

**T3 Ad, 30 s.** Normal: a person and a want (0-5 s). Explosion: the problem (5-15 s). Guide and plan: the product as the tool, never the hero (15-24 s). New normal: a specific result and one call to action (24-30 s).

**T4 Talk, 10 to 20 min.** Open a personal story at its peak (loop A); set "what is" against "what could be"; alternate them with evidence and a second story (loop B); deliver the core idea and one unforgettable moment; close B, then A; end on the new bliss and the ask.

**T5 Article.** Three-sentence ABT lede; one case told as a nested loop; a turn where the obvious answer fails; resolution; close on a concrete image, not a summary.

## 4. Modern creator storytelling

### 4.1 Kallaway [partly verified]

Kallaway (his handles give the name Kane Kallaway) runs a short-form strategy channel with about 462K YouTube subscribers as displayed on 2026-09-26 [S55]. His February 2026 "Storylocks" video lists six tactics: term branding, embedded truths, thought narration, negative frames, loop openers and contrast words. In the contrast segment (checked from auto-captions) he says curiosity comes from a gap between what the viewer expects and what the creator believes, that "but" is the strongest contrast word, and that "actually", "instead", "turns out", "except" and "yet" do the same job: "Contrast is the single most reliable way to create curiosity" [S55]. The "so" half of his but/so logic matches Parker and Stone's "therefore" [S39]; his wording for it was not checked [Unverified], nor were other videos such as "The Neuroscience of Addictive Storytelling".

Takeaway: short-form story is a chain of expectation breaks. Each "but" opens a gap; each "so" cashes it into consequence.

### 4.2 Philipp Humm and Vishen Lakhiani [content unverified]

Philipp Humm teaches storytelling to creators on YouTube (for example "Give me 9min, and I'll improve your storytelling skills by 176%"; not reviewed) [S56]. Vishen Lakhiani founded Mindvalley in 2002 and wrote two bestselling books [S57]; his "dopamine and suspense tricks" could not be checked [Unverified]. The tricks usually sold under that label (open a story at its peak, promise a payoff and hold it back, pause before the reveal, call back at the end) work through curiosity and suspense (2.2, 2.4), not dopamine doses (2.6).

### 4.3 Johnny Harris and documentary YouTube

Johnny Harris made the Emmy-nominated Borders series for Vox, won an Emmy for a New York Times Opinion video essay, has run his own channel since 2020 (about 7.96 million subscribers) and co-founded Newpress in 2026 [S58]. Our reading of his format [Craft, not a sourced claim]: each video is a question he is personally trying to answer; the researcher is the character; maps and documents are the setting; findings are staged as discoveries, often with a midpoint that overturns the first answer.

Education research supports the "misconception first" opening used by science channels: Derek Muller (later of Veritasium) found that multimedia which states and then confronts common misconceptions produced more learning than a clear, straight explanation, although students rated the straight version clearer [S59; reported from the paper, abstract not retrievable in this session].

### 4.4 Finding the story in a factual topic

Answer five questions before writing:

1. **Character:** who or what wants something? A person is best; a group, place or object can stand in.
2. **Question:** what single question will the viewer want answered by the end?
3. **Conflict:** what is in the way: nature, a rival, a wrong belief, time, money?
4. **Turning point:** where does expectation break (McKee's gap)?
5. **Reveal:** what does the viewer learn at the end that changes how they see the start?

No answer to 1 or 3 means you have a list of facts: go find a person or a wrong belief.

Illustrative example (invented): a bakery that sells out by 9 a.m. Character: Mira, the owner. Question: why not bake more? Conflict: one oven and an 18-hour rise. Turning point: when she doubled the batch, quality dropped and sales fell. Reveal: the small batch and the queue are the product. ABT: "Mira's bakery sells out by 9 every morning, AND the queue grows every week, BUT when she baked twice as much the bread got worse and sales dropped, THEREFORE she kept the batch small and made the queue part of the brand."

The and-then version of the same facts ("she opened a bakery, and then it got busy, and then she baked more, and then she went back") has no reason to keep listening. As a 40-second short (T1): 0-2 s, an empty shelf at 8:55 and the line "This bakery turns people away every morning, on purpose." 2-8 s: one oven, 200 loaves. BUT last spring she doubled it. SO the oven ran all night. BUT the bread came out pale and sales fell. SO she went back to 200. Payoff: people come for the queue as much as for the bread. Close on the same shelf at 9:01.

## 5. Devices

| Device | How to use it | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Hook as a question | Open a specific gap in the first line; invite a guess | Curiosity triggers [S4]; YouTube reports the share still watching at 30 s [S54] |
| Stakes | Say what is lost, for whom, by when. Dicks's tools: elephant (visible need early), backpack (share the plan and hopes first), breadcrumbs (hints), hourglass (slow down before the key line), crystal ball (a prediction that may prove wrong) | [Craft] [S45] |
| Conflict | Every scene has a gap between expectation and result; wins come with a cost ("yes, but") | [S42] |
| Contrast | Before and after, belief and reality, what is and what could be; contrast words | Content, visual and vocal contrast [S48]; [S55] |
| The unexpected | Break a schema, then explain it | [S49]; curiosity boosts memory for surprising answers [S5] |
| Foreshadowing | Plant early what pays off later; cut anything planted and never used | Chekhov's loaded rifle [S50] |
| Callback | Return to the opening image, line or joke with new meaning | [Craft] |
| Rule of three | Two items set a pattern, the third breaks it or lands the point | Comic triple; three-part lists draw applause (Atkinson 1984) [S53] |
| Cliffhanger and re-hook | End each section on an open question; restate the main question at the midpoint | Urge to resume [S7]; suspense [S8]; retention dips [S54] |
| Show, don't tell | One detail that implies the feeling; tell only to skip ahead | Chekhov's moonlight in a glinting shard of glass; Card and Bell on when telling is better [S51] |
| Dialogue | Quote a real line instead of paraphrasing; cut greetings and filler | [Craft] |
| Sensory detail | One precise sensory detail per scene; stacked adjectives become purple prose | Concreteness [S21]; Horace's "purple patches" [S52]; Cron warns against detail that does not serve the story [S44] |
| Pacing and rhythm | Vary sentence length; short lines at the peak; slow down before the reveal; vary pitch, pace and pause when spoken | Vocal contrast [S48]; Gary Provost's passage on sentence length [B3] |

## 6. Testing a draft

### 6.1 Tension map (our method) [Craft]

1. Split the script into beats: one new piece of information, action or change each (about one sentence in short-form; 15 to 45 s in long-form).
2. Score each beat 0 to 3 on four rows:
   - **Q, open question:** does the viewer want to know something right now?
   - **S, stakes:** do they know what someone they care about could lose?
   - **U, uncertainty:** could the next beat plausibly go more than one way?
   - **P, payoff:** did this beat change something (reveal, reversal, decision, a fact that matters)?
3. Tension T = Q + S + U (0 to 9). Plot T as a line and P as bars across the runtime.
4. Read the shape:
   - **Flat zone:** T under 4 for more than two beats (short-form) or about 45 s (long-form). Cut, or add a BUT.
   - **Orphan payoff:** P of 2 or more with no T of 5 or more before it. The reveal lands on nothing; set it up earlier.
   - **Unpaid tension:** T of 6 or more for a long stretch with no P. Pay off a smaller loop.
   - **Section end:** the last beat of each section needs Q of 2 or more.
   - **Overall:** peaks climb; the highest T sits just before the biggest P, around 75 to 90% of runtime.
5. After publishing, lay YouTube's retention graph over the map: dips mark skipped or abandoned moments, spikes mark rewatches [S54]. A dip at a high-T beat usually means an unclear question or a broken promise.

### 6.2 Quick tests

- **"So what" test:** after each beat ask "and so?"; if nothing follows, cut or connect it (Cron's "And so?" test [S44]).
- **Stakes check:** one line each for who wants what, what failure costs, and why the viewer cares.
- **Surprise count:** beats where belief moves. Short-form: at least one real surprise; long-form: one every two to three minutes. Each must be fair, set up earlier.
- **ABT audit:** label each transition AND-THEN, BUT or THEREFORE. Olson's Narrative Index compares "but" with "and" [S38]; our threshold: more than one AND-THEN in three means an episodic draft.
- **Loop ledger:** every question, tease and promise with open and close times; all close; the main loop closes last.
- **Dinner test:** would you say it this way to a friend at dinner? If not, rewrite [S45].
- **Table read:** read aloud with two or three people, or through TTS at final speed; mark stumbles, drift and lines nobody would say.

## 7. How stories fail

- **Story as decoration:** an anecdote you could delete without changing the argument. Fix: the turning point must be the evidence for the claim or the reason for the ask.
- **Fake stakes:** "this changes everything" with nothing specific at risk; hooks never paid. Fix: name the concrete loss; pay every promise.
- **Melodrama:** emotion bigger than its cause; adjectives and music doing the work. Fix: understate, use one exact detail, let the viewer feel it.
- **AI-generated tells.** LLM stories come out homogeneously positive and short on tension and turning points [S60]; they pass three to ten times fewer creative-writing tests than professionals' stories, and LLM judges fail to see it [S61]; professional editors flag cliches, unnecessary exposition, purple prose, poor sentence structure, lack of specificity, tense inconsistency and awkward word choice [S62]; AI stories offer less imaginative scenarios [S63]; AI-assisted stories resemble each other more [S64]. Look for:
  1. Predictable arc: problem, effort, quick success, lesson; no reversal, no cost.
  2. Generic characters: stock names, no contradiction, no specific flaw.
  3. The moral at the end: a closing line that states the lesson.
  4. Stock phrases: "little did they know", "in a world where", "a testament to", "journey", "tapestry".
  5. Exposition explaining what the scene just showed.
  6. Emotions named instead of shown.
  7. Reflexive triplets and "it's not X, it's Y" lines (Wikipedia's rule-of-three article cites research on LLM overuse of triplets [S53]; that study was not checked).
  8. Tidy symmetry: every thread tied with a bow, conflicts solved by a sudden realisation.

## 8. Implications for our skill

### 8.1 Rules

- **R1** Find the story before drafting: character, question, conflict, turning point, reveal (4.4). If character or conflict is missing, find one; never invent one in factual work.
- **R2** Chain beats with BUT or THEREFORE; an AND-THEN transition needs a reason.
- **R3** Open the first loop in the first line (short-form: first 2 s; long-form: first 15 to 30 s) with a question, a contrast or an in-medias-res moment, plus enough context to see the gap.
- **R4** Keep a loop ledger. Close every loop, the main one last. Never promise what the script does not pay.
- **R5** State concrete stakes (who loses what) before the midpoint.
- **R6** Every section carries a name, place, number or object. Keep statistics out of the emotional peak.
- **R7** Put the key message in or right after the biggest emotional or surprise peak.
- **R8** Choose the emotional shape on purpose: man in a hole by default; Icarus or Oedipus for cautionary tales; kishotenketsu when there is no villain.
- **R9** One midpoint reversal in long-form; one reframe in short-form.
- **R10** End on shown change, not a stated moral; a callback beats a summary.
- **R11** No pop-science claims: no oxytocin, dopamine doses, "22 times" or goldfish.
- **R12** Factual scripts: no invented people, quotes or events; label composites and hypotheticals.

### 8.2 Templates

Use T1 to T5 (3.1). Every template output ships with its loop ledger and tension map.

### 8.3 Automatic checks

C1 transition audit (flag AND-THEN above one in three); C2 loop ledger (all closed, main loop last); C3 first-loop timing (R3); C4 a concrete stakes line; C5 specificity density (names, numbers, places, concrete nouns per 100 words); C6 surprise count against the format target (6.2); C7 moral-ending detector (lesson phrasing in the last two sentences); C8 AI-tell lint (section 7 phrase list, triplets, "not X but Y", named emotions); C9 tension-map flags (6.1); C10 rhythm (sentence-length variance, read-aloud time at target words per minute).

### 8.4 Judge criteria (1 to 5 each)

- **J1 Story:** a character with a want, an obstacle and a change.
- **J2 Hook:** a specific, visible gap at the start; the payoff matches it.
- **J3 Causality:** beats chain by but and therefore.
- **J4 Stakes:** concrete and felt, not announced.
- **J5 Tension shape:** no flat zones, rising peaks, a strong late payoff.
- **J6 Surprise:** at least one fair surprise that changes understanding.
- **J7 Specificity:** concrete detail, shown not told, no purple patches.
- **J8 Ending:** change shown, a callback, no moral line.
- **J9 Voice:** passes the dinner test and table read; no AI tells.
- **J10 Truth:** in factual work every claim sourced; no invented quotes or people.

Pass: every criterion 3 or more, average 4 or more, J10 at 5 for factual work. Run the judge as a separate model call from the writer, give it the script and the tension map, and spot-check with humans, because LLM judges agree poorly with experts on creative quality [S61].
