# R4. Writing blog posts and articles (2025 to 2026)

Research file for the professional writing skill. Compiled 2026-09-26.

How to read it: [IDs] point to the source appendix after section 10 (G Google, S studies, N Nielsen Norman Group, E editorial and practitioner, T trade press). "Secondary" means reported by a third party, "unverified" means no primary confirmation, "heuristic" marks our own working rule. The web-search budget ran out midway, so later sources were reached by direct URL.

## 1. Ten findings in one screen

1. Google's bar is non-commodity content: first-hand, expert or original material beyond common knowledge. Google says it likely matters more for AI search than anything else in its guide [G7].
2. Scaled content abuse is judged by purpose and value, not by the tool; mass AI pages, stitched content and a page per query phrasing are spam [G3][G7].
3. The public rater guidelines (11 September 2025) rate low-effort copied or AI-paraphrased content Lowest, fake AI author profiles deceptive and filler before the answer Low [G2].
4. AI summaries cut clicks (8% vs 15% [S1]; position one CTR down 58% [S3]); being cited softens the loss [S4].
5. Google's myth list: no llms.txt, chunking, special schema, AI-specific rewriting or seeded mentions [G7].
6. GEO lab gains from citations, quotes and statistics [S7] did not carry over to most conversational SEO tricks [S8]; evidence-rich writing is the overlap.
7. Readers scan: 20 to 28% of words read [N4], the first 10 seconds decide [N12], heading-led scanning works best [N2].
8. FAQ rich results ended on 7 May 2026 [G18]; schema is optional for AI features [G7].
9. AI tells are fixed with substance (experience, sourced numbers, named examples, a position), not synonym swaps [E8][G2].
10. Conversion works when the product does a real step [E12], offers fit the post [E16] and disclosures sit by the link [E26].

## 2. Google Search: what the primary sources say

### 2.1 People-first content

Google's self-assessment (updated 2025-12-10) asks whether a page [G1]:
- offers original information or analysis beyond the obvious;
- adds substantial value over the other results;
- has a title that describes without exaggerating;
- is worth bookmarking or citing, and free of easily verified errors.

Warning signs of search-first content [G1]: writing to a word count (Google has no preferred count), mostly summarizing others, entering a niche for traffic without expertise, promising answers that do not exist (an unconfirmed release date), changing dates without real edits, and leaving readers needing to search again.

Who, How, Why [G1]. Who: bylines linked to author pages where readers expect them. How: explain how the work was done (products tested, method, photos) and disclose automation where readers would wonder. Why: to help people, which Google calls the most important question. An AI byline is probably not the right way to show AI involvement [G5].

### 2.2 E-E-A-T and the rater guidelines

- E-E-A-T is not itself a ranking factor. Google's systems use signals that line up with it, weight it more on YMYL topics (health, money, safety, society) and treat trust as the core [G1]. Experience was added on 2022-12-15 [G24].
- The public PDF was still dated 11 September 2025 when downloaded on 2026-09-26 [G2]; that version added AI Overview rating examples and a wider civics YMYL definition [T2]. Blog claims of a "June 2026" revision (a "Synthetic Authority" flag and so on) do not match its table of contents: unverified [T3].
- Sections to design against [G2]:
  - 4.6.6: Lowest when nearly all main content is copied, paraphrased, AI-generated or reposted with little effort, originality or added value. AI use alone does not decide the rating.
  - 4.5: invented author profiles or AI photos that make AI content look human-written are deceptive.
  - 5.2.2: the most helpful content belongs at the top; filler ahead of it rates Low (their example: a craft tutorial opening with well-known facts about supplies).
  - 3.4 and 3.4.1: experience means first-hand or life experience; on YMYL topics it can rate high when safe and consistent with expert consensus, but some advice must come from experts.
  - 12.6 and 12.7: intents are Know (including Know Simple), Do, Website and Visit-in-person; users are assumed to want current information.
- Rater scores do not move individual rankings [G1].

### 2.3 Spam policies that touch writers (page updated 2026-08-28)

- Spam now explicitly includes attempts to manipulate generative AI responses in Search [G3].
- Scaled content abuse: many pages made mainly to manipulate rankings, "no matter how it's created": AI pages without added value, scraped text spun through synonyms or translation, stitched content, keyword-laden nonsense [G3]. Separate pages for every phrasing of a query, made to sway rankings or AI answers, count too [G7].
- Site reputation policy (formerly "site reputation abuse"): third-party content hosted mainly to borrow a host's ranking signals. From 2026-08-30, after talks with the European Commission, manual actions stop applying for EEA searchers (the section may be ranked separately instead) but still apply elsewhere. Reviewers weigh presentation, quality consistency, stated authorship and duplication elsewhere; freelance work under clear editorial oversight is unlikely to be actioned [G3][G4].
- Also relevant: keyword stuffing, thin affiliation (merchant copy plus affiliate links), scraping and, since April 2026, back button hijacking [G3].

### 2.4 Google on AI-generated content

- Appropriate AI use is not against the guidelines; automation used mainly to manipulate rankings is spam [G5]. AI may help research and structure; titles, meta descriptions, alt text and markup meet the same accuracy bar; readers should learn how content was made; Merchant Center requires IPTC labels on AI images and labels on AI product text [G6]. Write from what you know, not what a model could easily produce [G7].
- Graphite estimated AI-written articles passed half of new English articles in November 2024, then plateaued, yet rarely appear in Google or ChatGPT results (detector-based, since revised down by the authors) [S13]. Ahrefs' detector classed 87.8% of pages cited in AI Overviews as mixed human and AI, 3.6% as pure AI [S12].
- Reading: assisted writing is normal; unedited output rarely wins. Writing is thinking [E23], so the skill must supply angle, evidence and judgment, not only sentences.

### 2.5 Core updates 2024 to 2026

Google's status dashboard lists nine core updates from March 2024 (45 days) to May 2026 (21 May to 2 June) and eight spam updates, the latest starting 24 September 2026 [G10].

- March 2024 folded the helpful content system into core ranking; Google later reported 45% less low-quality, unoriginal content against a 40% goal [G11][G12]. August 2024 aimed to surface more original work from small and independent sites [G13].
- Recovery advice: no single fix, no quick hacks, improve before deleting, expect months [G14].
- After May 2026, Reddit's share of top-three results rose from 8.56% to 10.24%, mostly in experience-led niches, while YMYL niches barely moved (SE Ranking, 100,000 keywords) [S18]. Sites scaling low-quality AI translations dropped [S19]; an unconfirmed January 2026 update hit "commodity content" and self-serving listicles (secondary) [S20].
- For writers: match the kind of answer the query deserves (experience, community, video, authority) and add what the top results lack.

## 3. AI Overviews, AI Mode, ChatGPT and Perplexity

### 3.1 Clicks: what credible data shows

| Source | Data | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Pew Research Center, 2025-07 [S1] | 900 US adults, 68,879 searches, March 2025 | Regular result clicked on 8% of visits with an AI summary vs 15% without; 1% clicked inside the summary; 26% ended browsing vs 16%; 18% of searches showed a summary; Wikipedia, YouTube and Reddit cited most |
| Ahrefs, 2025-04 and 2026-02 [S2][S3] | 300,000 keywords | Position one CTR 34.5% lower, then 58% lower (December 2025 vs 2023) when an AI Overview shows |
| Seer Interactive, 2025-11 [S4] | 3,119 queries, 42 organizations | Organic CTR on AI Overview queries down 61% (other queries 41%); cited brands had 35% higher organic and 91% higher paid CTR |
| Reuters Institute, Chartbeat data, 2026-01 [S5] | 2,500+ publishers | Google organic referrals down 33% globally, 38% in the US (Nov 2024 to Nov 2025); Google sends about 500 times ChatGPT's referrals |
| Digital News Report, 2026-06 [S6] | Survey | 10% use AI chatbots for news weekly; 42% of them say they often click through (self-reported) |
| Google, 2025-08 [G15] | No public method | Organic clicks "relatively stable"; more engaged clicks; forums, video and first-hand views gain |

Reading: generic answers lose clicks; cited pages and pages with depth, experience, tools or data keep them. Search Console has reported AI feature impressions (not clicks) since June 2026, worldwide since 2026-08-31, plus a site-level opt-out [T1].

### 3.2 How pages get cited

- Google's AI features retrieve through its index and ranking systems and fan out into related sub-queries [G7][G8].
- 76.1% of AI Overview citations came from top-10 pages (Ahrefs, 1.9 million citations, July 2025) [S10]; across 75,000 brands, web mentions correlated most with AI Overview visibility (correlation only) [S11].
- ChatGPT, Perplexity and Gemini lean toward earned third-party media over brand-owned and social content [S9].
- The GEO paper (Princeton-led, KDD 2024): with a GPT-3.5 engine over top-five results for 10,000 queries, adding citations, quotations and statistics raised visibility up to about 40%; keyword stuffing failed; low-ranked sources gained most (citing sources: +115.1% at rank five, minus 30.3% at rank one) [S7].
- C-SEO Bench (2025): most conversational SEO methods were ineffective or harmful, ranking signals mattered more, and gains shrank as adoption spread [S8].

### 3.3 Myths vs evidence

| Claim | What the evidence says |
|---|---|
| Add llms.txt or Markdown copies | Google Search ignores them [G7] |
| Chunk pages into tiny blocks for AI | Not needed; "There's no ideal page length" [G7] |
| FAQ schema gets you cited | Schema is not required for AI features [G7]; FAQ rich results ended May 2026 [G18] |
| One page per long-tail variation | Unneeded; if built to manipulate, it is scaled content abuse [G7][G3] |
| Seed brand mentions | Inauthentic mentions do not help [G7]; earned mentions correlate [S11] |
| GEO boosts visibility by 40% | True in a 2023-era lab setup [S7]; conversational tricks did not hold up [S8] |
| AI referrals convert at 14.2% vs 2.8% | Vendor claim with no method: unverified [E19] |

What serves readers and answer engines alike: answer-first sections, headings that name what they answer, facts in text with names, units, dates and sources, material a summary cannot give, and one strong page per topic covering its fan-out questions [N3][G7][G8][G15].

## 4. Intent, briefs and information gain

### 4.1 Search intent and SERP analysis

- Label intent: Know, Do, Website, Visit [G2], or informational, commercial, transactional, navigational [E13].
- Read the top results for Ahrefs' "3 Cs": content type (post, product page, video), format (how-to, list, review, comparison) and angle (beginner, budget, current year) [E13].
- Note SERP features. If forums and video dominate, readers want lived experience and demonstration [S18].

### 4.2 How brief tools work and where they fall short

- Surfer: keywords and intent, audience and goal, headings from SERP analysis, a word-count range from competitors, NLP terms, competitor notes, internal links, examples, CTA [E17].
- Frase: keyword, intent, format, title, intro, SERP-based outline, conclusion or CTA [E18].
- Clearscope: grades topic and term coverage against top results and suggests questions and citations [E20].
- Limit: all three reverse-engineer what already ranks; Animalz named the result "copycat content" [E10]. Use tool output as a coverage floor, plan the gain separately and ignore word-count targets [G1][G7].

### 4.3 Information gain and first-hand experience

- Google's patent US11354342B2 (filed 2018, granted June 2022) scores the new information a document adds beyond what the user has already seen [G26]. Google has not confirmed it runs in ranking [E14], but its public guidance asks for the same thing [G1][G7].
- Sources of gain: original data or tests, first-hand experience, expert interviews, a clear position, underserved sub-questions [E14][E10].
- Experience signals from Google's review guidance: evidence of use (photos, audio, links), your own measurements, what sets an option apart, pros and cons from your own research, first-hand reasons for any "best" pick [G16].
- Google's contrast, paraphrased: generic tips for first-time homebuyers are commodity; a first-person account of skipping an inspection and what the sewer line revealed is not [G7].

## 5. Structure for how people read online

### 5.1 Reading evidence

- Users read at most 28% of the words on an average visit, more realistically 20% (45,237 page views) [N4].
- Visitors often leave within 10 to 20 seconds; pages that clear that bar hold people much longer [N12]. Chartbeat found 55% of pageviews got under 15 seconds of active attention (2014) [S17].
- 57% of viewing time falls above the fold, 74% in the first two screenfuls [N6].
- The F-pattern appears with unformatted text and low-commitment readers; front-loaded headings and paragraphs, bold key phrases, bullets and descriptive links break it [N1]. Layer-cake scanning (headings first, diving in where relevant) is the most effective pattern and needs good subheads [N2].
- Concise, scannable and objective text together raised measured usability 124% [N7].

### 5.2 Headlines and titles

- Google: descriptive, concise, distinct per page, no repeated keywords, main title the most prominent text; title links are cut to device width [G17]. Since a 2021 change Google uses the title element about 87% of the time [G27].
- Zyppy (80,959 titles): Google rewrote 61.6%; titles over 70 characters were rewritten 99.9% of the time; 51 to 60 characters had the lowest rate (39 to 42%); matching H1 and title helped [S15].
- NN/g: headlines must work out of context, carry useful information, skip cute idioms and front-load keywords [N11]; the first two words do most of the work [N5].
- Emotion: in 22,743 Upworthy A/B tests each extra negative word raised CTR 2.3% [S14], but Google warns against shocking titles [G1]. Use real stakes, not manufactured alarm.
- Numbers, questions and "power words" in headlines: the popular evidence is old vendor data; unverified.

### 5.3 Intros

- Give the answer or payoff in the first two or three sentences [N3][N1]; GOV.UK adds: do not repeat the summary in the first paragraph [E2].
- Backlinko's current guide uses PPP: preview the outcome, show proof (results, experience), then preview what is inside [E15]. The older APP formula (Agree, Promise, Preview) credited to Brian Dean is no longer on Backlinko's live guides: unverified.
- Animalz drafts BLUF: bottom line up front [E9].

### 5.4 Subheads, lists, tables, paragraphs, readability

- Subheads: descriptive, front-loaded, meaningful alone. GOV.UK prefers verb-led headings and avoids questions [E2]; question subheads can work on blogs when they mirror real queries (heuristic).
- Lists for parallel items or steps, with short bullets; callouts for key stats, quotes and definitions; bold no more than about 30% of text; summaries at the top of long pieces [N10]. Tables for options compared on the same attributes (heuristic).
- Paragraphs: GOV.UK caps them at five sentences and splits sentences over 25 words [E1]; Backlinko uses one or two sentences [E15].
- Reading level: grade 6 to 8 for general readers, 10 to 12 for experts, who also prefer plain language [N8]. Everyday words and active voice [E1]. Paul Graham argues simple writing is read more deeply and exposes empty ideas [E21].

### 5.5 Visuals and internal links

- People study images that carry information (real products, real people, charts) and ignore decorative stock [N9][N10].
- Alt text: describe what the image shows in context, no keyword stuffing; keep images near related text; descriptive file names [G22].
- Links: anchor text that makes sense alone (never "click here"), context around it, no chains of adjacent links, every important page linked from at least one other page, no magic number of links [G19].

### 5.6 FAQs and structured data (September 2026)

- FAQ rich results narrowed to authoritative government and health sites in August 2023 [G20] and stopped showing entirely on 2026-05-07 [G18]. HowTo rich results ended in September 2023 [G20]; seven more types (including Claim Review) were phased out in 2025 [G21].
- Structured data is optional for AI features and must match visible content [G7][G9].
- Keep Article or BlogPosting markup: headline, image, dates and author (a Person with name and url, one author per entry, nothing but the name in author.name) [G23].
- Keep an FAQ section only for questions readers really ask, answered in two to four sentences.

## 6. Editorial quality

### 6.1 Fact-checking and citations

- Google asks whether sourcing is clear and whether a page has easily verified errors [G1].
- Run SIFT on each claim: Stop; Investigate the source; Find better coverage; Trace the claim to its original context [E7].
- Link the primary source, give the date and sample for numbers, label vendor data as vendor data, mark estimates.
- Chicago's 18th edition (2024) asks authors to state whether and how far AI was used [E6].

### 6.2 Style guides

- AP: spell out one to nine, figures from 10; no serial comma in a simple series; abbreviate only Jan., Feb., Aug., Sept., Oct., Nov. and Dec. with dates [E5]; % sign with figures since 2019 [E4]. The 58th edition adds AI and criminal-justice chapters and a self-editing checklist [E3].
- Chicago 18th edition (2024): singular "they" accepted, guidance on citing AI output, shorter URLs in citations [E6]. Chicago favors the serial comma (not re-verified here).
- Pick one house style per client, record it in the brief, apply it consistently.

### 6.3 Editing passes

Animalz runs idea, angle, MECE outline, BLUF draft, revision (strip scaffolding, add authentic details), title last, then polish and proofread [E9]. A five-pass edit for the skill (heuristic, built from [E9][E3][E22]):

1. Structure: right answer, right format, clear thesis; cut sections that do not serve it.
2. Evidence: every claim sourced or experienced; remove unsupported superlatives.
3. Line: shorter sentences, specifics over abstractions, active voice, only hedges that carry meaning.
4. Copy: style guide, names, numbers, links, heading consistency.
5. Skim test: read only title, headings, first sentences and bold text. The argument should still come through.

### 6.4 How top blogs differ, and why

| Publication | Signature | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| Ahrefs | Product-led tutorials; topics scored for "business potential" 0 to 3; original data studies [E12][S2] | The product solves the reader's problem; data earns links and citations |
| Animalz | Opinionated, BLUF, anti-copycat [E9][E10] | Angle and expertise |
| Backlinko | PPP intros, one- or two-sentence paragraphs, many screenshots [E15] | Fast to scan, proof on screen |
| Wait But Why | Very long posts, stick-figure drawings, humor, deep research by a curious non-expert [E24][E25] | Story and jokes carry readers through hard topics |
| Paul Graham | Plain text, no images; says something true, important and new as strongly as it can [E22][E21] | Clarity and original thinking |
| Morning Brew | Short, witty, conversational items (my reading, unsourced); Business Insider's parent took a majority stake in 2020 [E28] | Voice as a daily habit |

No single house style wins; each works because the voice fits its readers and the substance is its own.

## 7. Conversion inside articles

- Match the CTA to intent: on informational posts the next step is a tool, template or deeper guide, not a demo request (heuristic).
- Product-led content: show the product doing a real step (screenshots), only on topics with business potential 2 or 3 [E12]; Animalz adds company by-products such as data, expertise and story [E11].
- Content upgrades: a post-specific bonus lifted one post's conversion from 0.54% to 4.82% (Backlinko, 2016, single site) [E16].
- Personalized CTAs converted 202% better than generic ones across 330,000 CTAs (HubSpot's own data) [E27]. The often-repeated HubSpot claim that in-text anchor CTAs drive most blog leads could not be located: unverified.
- Avoid full-screen interstitials [G28] and back button hijacking [G3]. Put affiliate or sponsor disclosures next to the recommendation, not only in a footer [E26]. Fake or AI-written reviews and testimonials are banned by the FTC's 2024 rule [E29].
- Placement: three CTAs at most: a soft offer after the first proof point, one in context where the product does a step, a concrete next action at the end (heuristic).

## 8. Failure modes and AI tells, with the editor's fix

| Tell | Why it fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Stock opener ("In today's digital landscape") | Filler before the answer [G2]; wastes the first 10 seconds [N12] | Open with the answer, a number or a real scene |
| Fluffy headings ("Unlocking the power of X") | Say nothing when scanned [N2][N11] | State the claim or the question answered |
| Keyword in every heading and paragraph | Reads as stuffing [G3]; engines understand synonyms [G7] | Title, H1 and intro once, then natural language |
| No first-hand detail | Commodity content [G7]; Low for raters [G2] | Add a test, measurement, screenshot, cost or mistake |
| "Experts say", "studies show" | Unverifiable [E8] | Name, link and date the source |
| AI vocabulary (delve, pivotal, testament, underscore, tapestry), "not just X but Y", triplets, em dashes | Recognizable; excess LLM vocabulary shows up across 15 million abstracts [S16][E8] | Plain verbs, varied rhythm, no em dashes |
| Trailing "-ing" analysis ("highlighting the importance of") | Fake depth [E8] | State the concrete consequence |
| Conclusion that restates the post | Adds nothing [E15] | End with a next action or decision rule |
| Invented experience or AI "author" persona | Deceptive for raters [G2]; fake testimonials break FTC rules [E29] | Use real inputs or say what was not tested |
| New date, same content | Google warning sign [G1] | Change dateModified only for substantive edits |

These are signs, not proof, and AI detectors are unreliable [E8]: fix the substance, not the surface.

## 9. Workflow, template, checklist and examples

### 9.1 Workflow

1. Define the reader, their task or decision, and what they already know.
2. Read the SERP: intent, 3 Cs, features, the AI Overview and its sources, forums and video. Note what every top result says (consensus) and what none says (gap).
3. Choose the gain: original data, a test, expert input, a worked example, a decision framework or a defensible position.
4. Gather evidence first: dated primary sources, screenshots, measurements, approved quotes, product steps.
5. Fill the brief (10.2); outline with claim-style H2s in reader-priority order.
6. Draft answer-first, one idea per paragraph; edit in five passes (6.3); fact-check with SIFT.
7. Package: title, meta description (no length limit; Google truncates it and often writes its own snippet [G25]), slug, alt text, internal links, Article markup, author box, disclosures.
8. Publish and measure (Search Console queries and AI impressions, engaged time, conversions); update when facts change, not to look fresh.

### 9.2 Outline template (how-to or explainer)

```
Title: [specific outcome or answer] for [reader or constraint]   (aim 50 to 60 characters)
Summary (1 to 2 sentences): the answer, plus what makes this page different (tested, data, experience)
Intro (40 to 100 words): answer first, one proof line, what is inside
Key takeaways (optional, 3 to 5 bullets)
H2 The answer or core method (steps or decision rule)
   H3 each step with evidence (screenshot, number, example)
H2 What happened when we tried it (first-hand data, surprises, mistakes)
H2 Options compared (table: option, best for, cost, drawback, verdict)
H2 When not to do this (edge cases, risks)
H2 Common mistakes (symptom, cause, fix)
   [in-context CTA where the product does a step]
H2 FAQ (only real questions, 2 to 4 sentence answers)
Close: next action or decision rule; 2 to 3 related links with descriptive anchors
Author box, disclosures (affiliate; AI use where expected), sources with dates
```

Variants: "best X" lists add test method, criteria and a first-hand reason per pick [G16]; comparisons lead with a verdict table; essays use thesis, argument and counterargument; data studies lead with the finding, then method and limits.

### 9.3 Pre-publish checklist

- [ ] Intent and format match the SERP, or the deviation is deliberate
- [ ] The gain fits in one sentence and is visible in the draft
- [ ] Answer in the first 100 words; headings alone tell the story
- [ ] Every number and claim sourced or experienced
- [ ] Short paragraphs; few sentences over 25 words
- [ ] Title specific, honest, 60 characters or fewer
- [ ] No banned phrases, em or en dashes, or keyword echo
- [ ] Descriptive links; informative visuals with alt text
- [ ] Byline, dates, disclosures and markup correct
- [ ] Close gives a next step; three CTAs at most

### 9.4 Weak vs strong (my own examples; figures are illustrative)

- Title. Weak: "The Ultimate Guide to Email Marketing in 2026: Everything You Need to Know". Strong: "Welcome Emails: The 5-Email Sequence We Kept After Testing 9".
- Intro. Weak: "In today's fast-paced digital landscape, email remains a crucial tool. In this article, we will delve into..." Strong: "Send the first welcome email within five minutes, keep it under 120 words and ask one question. Below are the five emails we kept after testing nine on 4,200 trial users, and the two that hurt activation."
- Heading. Weak: "Unlocking the Power of Segmentation". Strong: "Segment by signup source before job title".
- Evidence. Weak: "Experts agree subject lines matter." Strong: "The plain subject line ('Your login link') beat the clever one on three of four sends."
- Close. Weak: "In conclusion, welcome emails are essential for success." Strong: "This week, move email one to the five-minute mark and cut it to one ask. Check activation after 14 days before changing anything else."
- CTA. Weak: "Sign up for our amazing tool today!" Strong: "Copy the five emails as a template (no signup), or build them in [Product]'s sequence editor, which sets the delays for you."
- Anchor. Weak: "click here". Strong: "our send-time test across 12 newsletters".
- Alt text. Weak: "email marketing tips email marketing". Strong: "Bar chart: activation by welcome email, highest after email 2".

## 10. Implications for our skill

### 10.1 Rules

1. Intent before outline: classify intent and format from the SERP; justify any deviation.
2. Gain is mandatory: original data, a first-hand test or experience, expert input, a worked example or a decision framework the top results lack. If inputs are missing, ask or insert [NEEDS INPUT: ...] markers. Never invent experiences, numbers, quotes, customers, authors or photos.
3. Answer first: the answer or payoff sits in the first 100 words; no scene-setting filler.
4. Sourcing: every statistic has a named source, link and date; primary preferred; vendor data labeled; never "studies show".
5. Titles: specific and honest, aim for 60 characters or fewer (hard limit 70), no keyword repetition, H1 consistent with the title.
6. Headings: descriptive, front-loaded, readable alone; the main keyword in title, H1 and intro, and in at most two subheads.
7. Rhythm: paragraphs of 1 to 4 sentences; average sentence under 20 words; split sentences over 25; grade 6 to 8 for general readers, 10 to 12 for experts.
8. Format: lists for parallel items, tables for comparisons, callouts for key stats, bold sparingly; no word-count targets.
9. Links and visuals: descriptive anchors, useful internal links, primary external sources; informational images with descriptive alt text.
10. Markup: Article or BlogPosting with the real author and dates; no FAQ rich result promises; no llms.txt or "AI markup" work for Google.
11. Conversion: CTA fits intent; product only where it performs a step (business potential 2 or 3); three CTAs at most; disclosure next to affiliate links; no fake urgency.
12. Transparency: a real human byline from the client; disclose AI help where readers would expect it; never an AI byline or invented persona. YMYL topics need a named expert author or reviewer [G2].
13. Dates: change dateModified only for substantive edits; a year in the title only for year-specific content.
14. Style: the client's guide, else AP for blogs; no em or en dashes; plain words, active voice; close with a next action, never a recap.

### 10.2 Brief template (filled before drafting)

```
topic | primary query | secondary questions (People Also Ask, forums, fan-out)
reader: who, situation, knowledge level, job to be done
intent (Know / Do / Website / Visit) and 3 Cs (type, format, angle)
SERP consensus (what all top results say) and gap (what none say)
information gain: which asset (data, test, expert, example, framework) and its source
evidence: sources with dates; first-hand inputs; items marked NEEDS INPUT
outline (H2 claims); visuals needed; internal links (target and anchor)
conversion: business potential 0 to 3; CTA type and placement; disclosures
voice and style guide; author and reviewer; YMYL flag (expert review needed?)
```

### 10.3 Automated checks

- Zero U+2014 (em dash) and U+2013 (en dash) characters.
- Banned phrases (starter list from [E8] and section 8): "in today's", "digital landscape", "ever-evolving", "fast-paced world", "delve", "tapestry", "testament to", "pivotal", "underscore", "unlock the power", "game-changer", "navigate the complexities", "it's important to note", "in conclusion", "look no further", "whether you're a", "seamless", "elevate". Flag "not only ... but also" and runs of three adjectives.
- Title: pass up to 60 characters, warn 61 to 70, fail above 70. Exact keyword in more than two subheads: fail.
- Over 15% of sentences above 25 words: warn. Paragraphs over 4 sentences: warn; over 6: fail.
- A figure (digits with %, currency or "study") with no link or named source in its paragraph: fail.
- Anchor text "click here", "here", "read more" or "this article": fail.
- Affiliate link without a nearby disclosure: fail. Leftover NEEDS INPUT markers: block delivery.
- Markup: author.name holds only a name; ISO 8601 dates; datePublished not after dateModified.

### 10.4 Judge rubric

Score 1 to 5 per criterion against a short SERP summary, blind to the brief's claims about the draft:

| Criterion | Weight | A 5 looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Intent match | 15 | Right format and angle; answer within the first 100 words |
| Information gain | 20 | The judge can name one useful thing absent from the top three results |
| Experience and evidence | 20 | Concrete first-hand details; every claim sourced; no errors |
| Structure and clarity | 15 | Headings alone tell the story; easy to scan; plain language |
| Human voice | 10 | Specific, varied, on-brand; none of the section 8 tells |
| Trust and transparency | 10 | Real byline, honest title, dates, disclosures |
| Conversion fit | 10 | The next step feels like help; the product appears where it does work |

Pass: weighted mean of 4.0 or higher and no criterion below 3. Hard fails whatever the score: a fabricated fact, figure, quote, persona or experience; an unsourced statistic; a misleading title; an undisclosed affiliate relationship; a near-duplicate page made for a query variation; any em or en dash.

### 10.5 Re-check each quarter

The rater guideline PDF date; the spam policy page date; whether the Search Console AI report adds clicks; Google's list of supported structured data; notes on the latest core update; any replication of GEO results on current engines.
