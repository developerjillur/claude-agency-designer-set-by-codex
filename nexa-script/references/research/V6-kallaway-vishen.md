## V6.1 Kallaway: "Say This in Your Videos, It'll Improve Your Storytelling by 10x" (`pcnrzBwoVUk`, 19:13)

### 1. What it teaches, as a numbered method

The premise [0:03 to 0:37]: attention depends on the words you say more than on topic or editing; you do not need to be a good writer, only to know six sets of words he calls "storylocks"; swap a few words in any script and it becomes more "addictive".

**1. Term branding [0:53 to 3:03]**
- Rule: give a core concept a name, not only an explanation [1:00].
- Claimed mechanism: a "labeling effect" makes named things feel important [1:09]; an unfamiliar name makes the viewer ask what it is, and that anticipation holds them until it is explained [2:01 to 2:16].
- Examples: Hormozi named his offer advice the "value equation" [1:19]; Kevin Kelly's "1,000 True Fans" outlived any essay on loyal audiences [1:29 to 1:41]; his own coined names (Lego brick framework, content minutes, audience matching, trust ladder) act as "digital trademarks" [1:44 to 1:56].
- Proof inside the video: he points out two coined terms already used in the first 90 s, "storylocks" and "labeling effect" [2:21 to 2:36].
- Tactic: before publishing, ask whether the core idea can be packaged under a name that makes it easier to grasp [2:40].
- Guardrail: not everything needs a name; use it for contrarian ideas and your best frameworks [2:51].

**2. Embedded truths [3:03 to 5:59]**
- Rule: state things as already true instead of offering them as an option [3:10].
- Core pair: "if you try this" versus "when you try this" [3:19]. "If" gives the viewer a choice, a fork in the road where attention leaks; "when" leaves one road [3:23 to 3:46].
- Three more pairs [3:57 to 4:05]: "this might work because" becomes "the reason this works so well"; "you might notice" becomes "once you see it, you can't unsee it"; "some creators might be making a mistake" becomes "the mistake most creators make is".
- Claimed mechanism: every if, maybe or might is a micro-moment of doubt, a small exit door for attention [4:26 to 4:37].
- Example: a Shot by Sammy clip [4:52 to 5:11], praised because a percentage and a "psychological cheat code" are stated as fact [5:11 to 5:30].
- Tactic: delete if, maybe, might, could, probably and anything non-committal; replace with when, the reason why, once you do this, this is how it works [5:30 to 5:47]. If a line sounds like hedging, remove the hedge [5:47 to 5:59].

**3. Thought narration [6:26 to 8:48]**
- Rule: say out loud what the viewer is thinking at that moment [6:32].
- Demonstration, then reveal: he voices the viewer's likely doubt that anyone could read their mind, then points out that he just did it [6:41 to 6:57].
- His stock lines [7:03 to 7:17], paraphrased: the question you're probably asking now is this; if I were you, the thing on my mind would be this; if one thing worries you, it's probably this.
- Claimed benefits [7:17 to 8:09]: the viewer trusts you because you knew what was in their head; they must keep watching for your answer; their inner monologue becomes a one-on-one conversation ("personalization at scale"); it breaks the frame and makes people refocus.
- Example: Hormozi stops mid-explanation to voice the viewer's doubt that the idea could work for them, then answers it [8:09 to 8:23].
- Tactic: after each major point, ask what the viewer should be thinking right now and say it at a transitional moment. A few times per video, not constantly [8:26 to 8:46].

**4. Negative frames [8:48 to 11:32]**
- Rule: frame a point as a threat or a mistake instead of a gain.
- Demonstration: "how to build a strong personal brand" against "the worst thing you could do for your personal brand" [8:55 to 9:10].
- Claimed mechanism: the brain favours threats; he names loss aversion [9:10 to 9:20] and cites research that people are "twice as motivated to avoid pain" [9:21]. The viewer switches from opting in to learning to protecting themselves, which is more urgent [9:57 to 10:05].
- Trigger phrases: stop doing this; this is a warning; don't make this mistake [9:27].
- Three creator hooks [9:38 to 10:41]: posting daily called the worst thing for your brand, followed by a "hear me out, it sounds backwards" line; a triple "never" warning about an Instagram feature, which he says was deliberate and then copied [10:07 to 10:24]; a softer frame saying you make content harder than it needs to be, which implies a mistake without accusing [10:26 to 10:41].
- Technique: "inverting", which he brands the "negative flip" [10:45 to 11:10]: use this hook format becomes stop making your hooks like this; how to grow on YouTube becomes this is killing your YouTube growth; post more consistently becomes this posting mistake is costing you followers.
- Tactic: when a good point does not land, flip it negative [11:17]. Guardrail: sometimes the positive frame is right [11:25].

**5. Loop openers [11:32 to 14:37]**
- Model: each viewer's attention is an hourglass that starts on the first frame. A hook flips it once. A middle that only delivers facts lets it run out, which is the steady slope of a decaying retention graph [11:41 to 12:33].
- Definition: a line that flips the hourglass again by opening a new curiosity loop as the last one closes; he calls this "re-hooking" [12:36 to 12:49].
- Cadence claimed: every 20 to 30 s in shorts, every 60 to 90 s in long videos [12:49].
- Formula: confirm that what was just covered was valuable, then tease that the next part is valuable for a different reason [12:55 to 13:05].
- Examples [13:16 to 13:29]: here's the thing, though; that's not even the most interesting part; this is where it gets crazy; most people stop here, but; here's what nobody talks about; that was powerful, but the next one matters more.
- Detail: the best ones carry a contrast word in the middle (but, though) [13:40 to 13:55].
- Tactic: put one after every major section, like a bridge; five sections need three or four [14:04 to 14:21]. Claimed result: the retention graph stops dropping mid-video [14:21].

**6. Contrast words [14:37 to 17:58]**
- Claim: contrast is the most reliable source of curiosity and the universal principle of storytelling [14:42 to 14:53].
- Mechanism: curiosity is the gap between what the viewer expects and what the speaker believes; contrast words build that gap [14:56 to 15:08].
- "But" is the strongest [15:12]. Patterns [15:19 to 15:37]: A, but B; doing A, but also B; doing A, but B instead; you might think A, but actually B.
- Siblings, each with a job [15:37 to 16:20]: actually (what you thought was wrong), instead (redirect to an alternative), turns out (a reveal), except (an exception to an assumption), yet (a contradiction; he calls it old-fashioned).
- Unifying claim: contrast powers the other five devices [16:29 to 17:18]. A new name contrasts with what was expected, certainty contrasts with doubt, hearing your own thought contrasts with expectation, a warning contrasts with expected advice, a tease contrasts with what was just heard.
- Tactic: take each "clean point", the main punches of the script, and split it: lean the viewer one way, then turn with but or a sibling [17:24 to 17:44]. Guardrail: not every sentence; too many "but"s feel like friction [17:45 to 17:54].

**7. Stacking [18:23 to 18:41]**
- Do not force all six into every video, especially shorts; the more are layered, the stronger the pull. He brands the combination a "storytelling stack".

### 4. Weak, unproven or questionable advice

1. **"Labeling effect" [1:09].** Not an established name for "named things feel important". Real findings with similar names say other things: verbal labels change how ambiguous drawings are remembered (Carmichael, Hogan and Walter, 1932); naming an emotion dampens it ("affect labeling", Lieberman and colleagues, 2007); labeling theory in sociology is about stigma. What holds: a name is a handle that is easier to recall and reuse, and an unfamiliar name opens a small, visible knowledge gap (R3 2.2). His examples are survivorship bias: we remember names attached to strong ideas, not the thousands of named frameworks nobody recalls. "Works every single time" [2:16] and "they cannot leave" [2:13] are hype.
2. **Embedded truths [3:03].** The label and the "subconscious" talk come from NLP-style hypnotic language patterns (presuppositions, embedded commands); reviews find NLP's claims unsupported (for example Witkowski, 2010). What holds: presupposition in wording shapes answers (Loftus and Zanni, 1975: asking about "the" broken headlight produced more false reports than "a" broken headlight), and people prefer confident advisers (Price and Stone, 2004). What cuts the other way: once overconfidence is exposed, credibility drops below that of a calibrated source (Tenney and colleagues, 2007), and hedged science news can raise the credibility of scientists and journalists (Jensen, 2008). His model example is an unsourced statistic, "80% of visual storytelling is subconscious", praised precisely because it is stated as truth, even though its author softens it in the next breath. **That is the failure our system must block.** Verdict: commit in instructions and sourced claims, keep one honest caveat where evidence is thin, never state an unsourced number.
3. **Thought narration [6:26].** Sound in spirit. Refuting the audience's likely objection persuades better than ignoring it (refutational two-sided messages, O'Keefe's 1999 meta-analysis), and addressing viewers directly strengthens the feeling of a personal connection with a presenter (parasocial experience, Hartmann and Goldhoorn, 2011). Unproven: "they immediately trust you" and the "hypnotist" effect. Risk: a wrong guess pushes the viewer out ("no, I wasn't thinking that").
4. **Negative frames [8:48].** Loss aversion is real in choices about money (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979; a loss weight of about 2 in Tversky and Kahneman, 1992), and bad events outweigh good ones in many domains (Baumeister and colleagues, 2001). Negative words do raise clicks: across roughly 105,000 headline variants tested on Upworthy, each extra negative word raised click-through by about 2 percent (Robertson and colleagues, 2023). But "twice as motivated to avoid pain" [9:21] misstates the research: the factor of about 2 weighs monetary losses against equal gains in gambles, it shrinks or disappears for small stakes and in some designs (Gal and Rucker, 2018), and it does not measure motivation. Loss framing also does not reliably persuade more: meta-analyses of gain-framed against loss-framed health messages found little or no difference (O'Keefe and Jensen, 2007 and 2009). Clicks are not retention. His remark that the triple "never" was deliberate and then copied [10:13] is a guess. Risk: warning hooks on every video breed distrust, and fear hooks about health, money or religion can do real harm.
5. **Loop openers [11:32].** Curiosity gaps are well supported; the memory version of open loops is not (R3 2.2 and 2.3). The hourglass is a metaphor; no per-viewer timer has been measured. The 20 to 30 s and 60 to 90 s cadence and the "retention holds flat" result [14:21] come with no data. **Missing rule: pay off every loop.** A tease like "this is where it gets crazy" that does not deliver teaches viewers to ignore the next one, and his formula ("the next one is even more important") invites inflation when every item is hyped as bigger than the last.
6. **Contrast words [14:37].** The craft advice matches Parker and Stone's but/therefore and Olson's ABT (R3 section 3). "The single most reliable way" and "the universal principle" are overclaims, and recasting all five other devices as contrast [16:42] is a reframe after the fact that cannot be tested. He also drops the "therefore" half that turns a gap into consequence. The main risk for us: "split every clean point" mass-produces "It's not X, it's Y" lines, a known AI-writing tell (R3 section 7, item 7).
7. **Other overclaims.** "Not the topic, not the editing" [0:10] comes with no support, and topic choice drives views at least as much. The "10x" in the title is unmeasured. The community's closing enrollment [6:16] is urgency we cannot check.

## V6.2 Vishen: "Become a Master Storyteller (The Dopamine Trick Elite Speakers Use)" (`P-BQ-AGS0ck`, 25:55)

### 1. What it teaches, as a numbered method

The premise [0:00 to 0:34]: great storytelling makes people feel an idea, not only understand it. Speakers remembered years later bring emotion; others get applause and are forgotten.

**1. A memory device for the method [2:34 to 3:20]**
- Five elements, H-S-T-S-S, taught as a sentence ("Heroes start their stories somewhere") that the viewer repeats aloud [2:45 to 2:52], plus a profane version his students use [3:02 to 3:17]. The mnemonic is itself a term brand.

**2. H, the hook [3:20 to 8:17]**
- Definition: capture attention and leave people hanging [3:24].
- Main tool, **idea collision** [4:21 to 4:45]: put two ideas the mind never hears together into one sentence. His book opens with his nerves before sharing a stage with the Dalai Lama and a mid-day order of two vodka shots at the hotel bar [3:30 to 3:52]. A holy man and vodka collide, and readers keep going to find out what happened [4:03 to 4:19].
- Social timing [5:00 to 5:48]: citing Jay Shetty, hook in the first 3 s, deepen it until about second 11, then start the story. He rewrites the vodka opening as a 3-second line plus a short setup that ends on a forward pull ("this is what happened next").
- Talks and presentations: a captive room allows a longer hook [5:56 to 6:08].
- Interviews [6:10 to 8:15]: Naveen Jain, asked how he raised entrepreneurial children, rejected the idea that parents must spend all their free time with their kids and said he makes his kids watch him work. The collision is with standard parenting advice. He then explains with a contrast: children who see a parent watching TV learn to love TV; children who see a parent enjoying work learn that work can be meaningful [7:16 to 7:52]. Lesson: prepare a collision for every question you expect [8:03].

**3. S, the stakes [8:17 to 13:56]**
- Definition: the moment the hero might fail, when everything could collapse; film examples are a hand cut off in a duel and half of all life wiped out [8:27 to 8:52].
- Why it works: people attend to danger (he guesses it is wiring, so we learn from others' trouble) [8:52 to 9:08], and stakes add humility and make the speaker relatable [9:13 to 9:29].
- **Order: credibility first, then failure** [9:33 to 9:51]. He points out that he opened with his bestseller for exactly that reason, and now shows where he started.
- Demonstration story [10:15 to 12:15]: a computer science graduate in 1999 borrows $30,000 from his father, moves to Silicon Valley, the dot-com bubble bursts, the money is gone, and he ends up renting a two-seater couch from a Berkeley student for $200 a month; his legs hang off the end, there is a stain he covers with a towel, and he sends out résumés for months. It pauses on a cliffhanger: an email that changed his life.
- Rules [12:16 to 13:56]: show the lowest point; it must be true ("don't make things up") [12:54]; everyone has a moment of misery worth sharing [12:56 to 13:17]; describe how you felt, not only what happened, naming shame, guilt, anger [13:17 to 13:54]. His extra layer: he lied to his parents about where he lived because he could not ask his father for more money [13:24 to 13:45].

**4. T, the turn [13:56 to 18:35]**
- Definition: the pivot that changes the story, often with the lesson baked in [13:58 to 14:22].
- His turn [14:22 to 17:58]: a commission-only sales job (20% of what he closed, no base pay) and zero sales in the first month [14:26 to 15:05]; then the tone lifts with humour, his self-pitying search queries in 2001 [15:08 to 15:35]; he finds a meditation course and spends his last money to attend [15:36 to 16:07]; he calls leads by "intuition" [16:08 to 16:36]; sales double and he gets four promotions in three months [17:11 to 17:26]; his boss's reaction [17:26 to 17:58].
- Rules: the turn shares the discovery you want the audience to focus on [18:00 to 18:17]; it brings levity and takes the audience out of the dark with you [18:19 to 18:33].

**5. S, the scene [18:47 to 19:55]**
- Definition: make it visual so the audience is in the moment with you [18:59 to 19:06].
- Tools: weather, carpet, the room; the other person's gestures and face (the boss pounding the desk, his puzzled look) [19:24 to 19:38].
- Rule: do not rush a story; tiny details carry the audience in [19:40 to 19:55]. He adds that he has been building scenes all along [19:06 to 19:13].

**6. S, the shatter [19:59 to 21:24, and 23:52]**

### 2. Exact mechanics for checks and templates

- **Idea collision test.** The first sentence joins two ideas from domains that rarely meet: sacred and profane, high status and low, success and failure, expert and beginner. Both halves must be literally true.
- **Social timing.** 3 s is about 8 to 10 words at 170 to 200 words per minute; 11 s is about 30 to 37 words. His own 3-second line is 13 words (about 3.5 s as delivered), and his "11-second" version is about 58 words, which takes about 18 s at his average pace. Budget by word count, not by feel.
- **Stakes checklist** (his couch story passes all five): a concrete loss with a number (the $30,000); an image of the low point (the couch); a named feeling (shame); a secret or a cost (lying to his parents); true.
- **Turn checklist:** an event (the email, the job); a discovery; a change of tone (humour); a result with numbers (sales doubled, four promotions in three months); a witness who reacts (the boss).
- **Scene checklist:** place, object, body (a gesture), face, dialogue in direct speech; slow the delivery.
- **Shatter checklist:** one sentence; an old belief against a new one (hustle against stillness); placed before the evidence; closes the story.
- **Order:** proof line, then stakes, turn, shatter; scene details at the stakes and turn peaks.
- **Debrief line** in teaching videos: after each demonstration, "notice what I just did", then name the move.
- **Participation prompts:** grab a pen [2:28]; write this down [4:23]; repeat after me [2:49].

Measured in his script (25:55, 4,884 words):
- 188 words per minute overall. He slows to about 151 in the shatter section and 174 in the scene section, and speeds up to 218 in the CTA.
- "I/my" 11.5 per minute against "you/your" 7.3: a first-person storyteller, the reverse of Kallaway.
- 42 question marks (1.6 per minute), most of them rhetorical ("Why?", "What mental model do the children get?").
- "But" 1.3 per minute; almost no hedges.
- About nine explicit debriefs ("Now, notice what I did") across the video.
- Median sentence 11 words, 90th percentile 32: short lines with occasional long, run-on storytelling sentences.

### 4. Weak, unproven or questionable advice

1. **The "dopamine trick" is never mentioned.** Nothing in the 25 minutes concerns dopamine, brain chemistry or neuroscience; the only biology is his own guess that attention to danger "maybe" is wiring [8:55]. What is supported: dopamine neurons signal reward prediction errors (Schultz, Dayan and Montague, 1997); curiosity engages reward-anticipation circuits and improves memory for answers (R3 2.2); emotional arousal improves memory (R3 2.5). Pop science: that a story structure delivers "dopamine hits", or that elite speakers use a dopamine trick; no study measures this in audiences (R3 2.6). The title promises something the video never pays. Our scripts and titles must not do this.
2. **The social hook changes the facts [5:24].** The 3-second version says the Dalai Lama made him drink; in the story his own nerves did. A collision that rewrites what happened is clickbait. Rule: a hook must be literally true to the body.
3. **Credential inflation [1:36 to 2:19, 22:07, 23:16].** All self-reported. The book is "number one in the world" at 1:51 but "number one on Amazon" at 23:16, a much smaller claim. "The stickiest open rates" is never defined, and the book's success is credited to one technique with nothing to compare it against.
4. **The intuition proof [16:08 to 17:26].** An anecdote with no control. A new salesperson improves after a zero first month anyway (practice, confidence, regression from the worst month). Picking names from a phone book "by feel" gives intuition no real cues to use; expert intuition works where the environment offers valid cues and quick feedback (Kahneman and Klein, 2009). The "study" [16:59] is John Mihalasky's work at Newark College of Engineering, published with Douglas Dean and others in the 1974 book "Executive ESP": executives were tested on precognition, guessing numbers not yet generated. That is parapsychology that has not been replicated, not evidence about ordinary intuition. The Silva method behind the course has long claimed that a meditative state unlocks intuition and even psychic perception; there is no scientific support for that. Conflict of interest: the story ends in his own product, reported at $40 million a year, and the pitch is not flagged as one.
5. **"Nobody changes their life because of a fact" [23:40].** A strong line and a false claim. Narratives persuade with a small effect (Braddock and Dillard, 2016; R3 2.1), and meta-analyses comparing narrative with statistical evidence find statistics as strong or stronger for beliefs and attitudes (Allen and Preiss, 1997; Zebregs and colleagues, 2015), with narratives ahead on intentions in some cases. A shatter line may carry an opinion; it must not state a false fact.
6. **The Naveen Jain example [6:45 to 7:52].** It shows that a collision holds a room, but the parenting claim is unsupported, and Vishen concedes you may not agree. A collision needs a defensible claim behind it, or it is only provocation.
7. **Stakes psychology [8:52 to 9:51].** His ordering is the best-supported idea in the video. A blunder raises the likability of a competent person but lowers it for an average one (the pratfall effect, Aronson, Willerman and Floyd, 1966), and others judge shown vulnerability more kindly than the person expects (Bruk, Scholl and Bless, 2018). The relatability and respect pair matches warmth and competence, the two main dimensions of how people judge others (Fiske, Cuddy and Glick, 2007). The "biological wiring" explanation is his guess.
8. **Structure limits.** "Scene" is a texture that runs through the whole story, not a fourth step; he admits he had been building scenes all along. A rigid H, S, T, S, S order would put description after the turn. A stated lesson at the end conflicts with R3's no-moral rule (R10, C7); the synthesis below reconciles them. "Take your time" [19:44] has to be compressed for shorts.
9. **Minor.** The 3 s and 11 s rule is a practitioner heuristic with no data, and his own example overruns it. Profanity makes a mnemonic memorable (taboo words are recalled better: Jay, Caldwell-Harris and King, 2008) but carries brand risk.

## Synthesis for our skills

### 1. Two layers, one engine

Kallaway works on sentences; Vishen works on the story. Underneath, they use the same few mechanisms, and R3 already has the evidence for most of them. The layers meet in practice too: Vishen's first line is a Kallaway contrast (feel it, not just understand it), and his "but most people get storytelling wrong" is a Kallaway negative frame.

| Mechanism | Kallaway | Vishen | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Expectation gap | Contrast words, negative flip, an unfamiliar name | Idea collision, the turn | R3 2.2 (curiosity), 2.4 (surprise) |
| Loss | Negative frames | Stakes | Negativity bias (V6.1 section 4, item 4) |
| Presence | Thought narration, demonstrate then reveal | Scene, "notice what I did", participation prompts | Parasocial experience (V6.1 section 4, item 3) |
| Forward pull | Loop openers | A cliffhanger inside a running story | R3 2.3 (urge to finish), loop ledger |
| Certainty | Embedded truths | The mic-drop line | Calibration beats confidence (V6.1 section 4, item 2) |
| Memory handle | Term branding | Acronym mnemonic, one-line shatter | R3 2.7 (concreteness) |

Where they conflict, and our resolution:
- **Certainty against honesty.** Commit where the evidence is firm; calibrate once where it is not; never invent a number.
- **Stated lesson against "no moral".** One shatter line is allowed in teaching and keynote formats if it reverses a belief named earlier and the turn has earned it. Ads and fiction show the change and use a callback instead (R3 R10).
- **Speed against "take your time".** Shorts compress a scene to one or two details. Long-form slows down at the shatter, as Vishen does (151 words per minute there).
- **Negative frames against stakes.** Both are loss. A negative frame threatens the viewer; stakes threaten the character. Every story needs stakes; a piece needs at most one negative hook.

### 2. Rules the script skill should adopt

- **V6-R1 Story shape.** Any story segment has a hook, stakes, a turn, scene details and a closing line (a shatter or a shown change). Stakes come before the turn. Scene details sit at the stakes and turn peaks, not in a separate block.
- **V6-R2 Proof before vulnerability.** A narrator the audience does not know gets one proof line (10 s or less) before the stakes. After that, show the low point.
- **V6-R3 Stakes that are felt and true.** A concrete loss (a number, an object, a place) plus a named feeling. Composites and hypotheticals are labelled (R3 R12).
- **V6-R4 True hooks.** A hook may collide ideas but must be literally true to the body. The hook's key claim (who did what) must reappear accurately in the body.
- **V6-R5 Bridges.** Every section ends on a bridge line: acknowledge, turn with a contrast word, tease what is different about the next part. Every tease is paid. Never call the next part "even bigger" unless it is.
- **V6-R6 Calibrated commitment.** No hedges in the hook, instructions or CTA. "If" is for audience qualifiers only ("if you run a shop"); instructions use "when". Claims carry the confidence their claim-ledger entry supports: firm when sourced, one plain caveat when uncertain, cut when unsourced.
- **V6-R7 Thought narration.** Two to four times in long-form, at most once in shorts: after a big claim, in the viewer's words, answered immediately. Take the thought from real audience questions (comments, search queries, research), not from a guess.
- **V6-R8 Negative frames with a reason.** At most one negative hook per piece. Every warning names a real, common mistake. No fear hooks on health, money or religion unless the risk is sourced. Alternate positive and negative hooks across a series.
- **V6-R9 Term brands, few and defined.** One to three per long video, none or one per short. Define within two sentences, reuse at least twice, and never present an invented name as a psychological principle. For Bangla scripts, coin a Bangla name or gloss the English one.
- **V6-R10 Contrast with consequence.** Split main claims into expectation, then reversal, then consequence (but, then therefore; R3 R2). One to four contrast turns per spoken minute. At most one "not X, it's Y" per script.
- **V6-R11 The shatter line.** One per story in teaching or keynote formats: one sentence of 25 words or fewer, reversing a belief the script named earlier, placed before the evidence, and never a false factual absolute. No stock morals.
- **V6-R12 One scene the viewer can see.** Long-form has at least one scene with three or more concrete details (place, object, gesture, face, direct speech). Shorts use one or two. Delivery slows there.
- **V6-R13 Demonstrate, then reveal.** Teaching videos use the technique on the viewer at least once, then name it.
- **V6-R14 Packaging is paid.** The title, thumbnail and first line promise only what the script delivers. No "dopamine trick" packaging.
- **V6-R15 CTAs.** A mid-roll ask comes after at least two payoffs and never between stakes and turn. The end asks for two things at most. Urgency needs a real date. A story that leads to our own product says so.
- **V6-R16 Pop science.** Add "subconscious", "hypnotic", "cheat code", "brain is wired", "twice as motivated" and "labeling effect" (in Kallaway's sense) to the R3 R11 blocklist.

### 3. Templates

**T-V6a Teaching list (Kallaway shape, 8 to 20 min)**
1. Hook, 30 to 50 s: topic plus a desire word; contrast by elimination ("not A, not B: C"); a barrier removed; a number; a named set; a promise of ease; a roadmap (what, why, how).
2. Proof line, 10 s or less.
3. Items in rising order of value, each on the same sub-template: number and name > a different hype line > one-sentence definition > honest, sourced mechanism > example (famous, ours, or a clip) > why it matters here > a demonstration on the viewer where possible > tactic (on-screen card) > guardrail > bridge line.
4. Plant the last item inside the one before it; let the last item reframe all the others.
5. Mid-roll ask after item two (about 30% in).
6. Recap in one line per item; permission not to use everything; one end ask plus a next video.

**T-V6b HSTSS story**
- Short (45 to 60 s): 0 to 3 s, the collision line (8 to 10 words); 3 to 11 s, context that makes the collision make sense and pulls forward; 11 to 25 s, stakes (the low point, one feeling, one concrete detail); 25 to 40 s, the turn (an event and a lift in tone); 40 to 55 s, the shatter line; 5 s or less for the ask.
- Long (4 to 8 min): hook 20 to 40 s; proof line if needed; stakes 1.5 to 3 min with one expanded scene; turn 1 to 2 min with a witness who reacts; shatter in 20 s or less, then the evidence; close.

**T-V6c Running story inside a teaching video (Vishen shape)**
Pick one true story. Split it at cliffhanger points so each teaching section is one chapter. After each chapter, debrief ("notice what I just did") and teach the move. Resolve the story before the final lesson, then run a self-reveal that maps the video onto the method. Answer every rhetorical question the story raises.

**T-V6d Bridge line**
[Acknowledge what just landed] + [contrast word] + [tease a different benefit of the next part]. Pattern examples: "That fixes the opening, but it does nothing for the middle, which is where most viewers leave." "Most people stop at step two, but step three is where the money is." The teased part must pay.

**T-V6e Negative flip**
Positive advice > the cost of ignoring it. "Post consistently" > "This posting habit is costing you followers". "Use this hook" > "Stop opening with this". Allowed only when the mistake is real, common and checkable.

**T-V6f Commit rewrite with an honesty gate**
"This might help" > "Here's why this works" (only if the claim ledger says it works). "If you try this" > "When you try this". "Some people might" > "Most people" (only with data; otherwise "many people", or name the source).

**T-V6g Thought-narration slot**
After a major claim: [the viewer's most likely objection, in their words] > [an immediate, specific answer]. For example: "You're probably thinking this only works for big channels. It worked for a channel with 300 subscribers, and here's how."

**T-V6h Social 3 s and 11 s opener**
Sentence 1, 8 to 10 words: the collision, literally true. Sentences 2 and 3, up to about 35 words: the context that makes it make sense, ending on a forward pull. Check the timing against the real voice rate, not a guess.

### 5. What a judge should score

Score 1 to 5. These extend R3's J1 to J10. The judge receives the script, the loop ledger and the claim ledger, never the writer's notes.

| ID | Criterion | 5 looks like | 1 looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| V6-J1 | Hook truth and pull | The first line opens a specific gap (collision, contrast or a justified warning); literally true to the body; promise stated by 30 s (long) or 3 s (short) | A generic topic line, or a hook the body contradicts |
| V6-J2 | Promise and loop integrity | Every tease and question is paid, the main promise last; teases never oversell | Loops left open; "it gets crazy" with nothing crazy |
| V6-J3 | Bridges and rhythm | Every section ends with a forward pull; bridges vary in wording; no flat stretch | Sections simply stop; the same stock bridge every time |
| V6-J4 | Stakes | A concrete, true loss and a named feeling, after a proof line | No risk, or a boast with no low point |
| V6-J5 | Turn | A clear event that changes direction, carries the lesson and lifts the tone | No pivot, or sudden success with no cause |
| V6-J6 | Scene | At least one moment the viewer can see, slowed down, with a gesture or a spoken line | Summary only |
| V6-J7 | Closing line | One earned line that reverses a named belief and is true; or, in ads and fiction, a shown change and a callback | A stock moral, a false absolute, or no point |
| V6-J8 | Conversation | The viewer's real objections voiced and answered; natural direct address; a demonstration then reveal in teaching | A lecture with no viewer in it, or wrong mind-reading |
| V6-J9 | Calibrated confidence | Firm where sourced, one plain caveat where not; no invented numbers; no pop science | Invented statistics, or hedging everywhere |
| V6-J10 | Device restraint | Devices serve the content; few, defined names; contrast is not a tic; warnings justified | A visible formula, jargon soup, fear-mongering, chains of "not X, it's Y" |
| V6-J11 | Packaging honesty | Title, thumbnail and hook are paid; self-promotion disclosed; urgency real | Bait the video never pays, a hidden pitch, fake scarcity |

Pass: every criterion at 3 or more and an average of 4 or more. For factual work, J9 and J11 must be 5, and a hook that the body contradicts fails J1 outright.
