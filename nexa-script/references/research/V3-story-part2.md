## 0. Findings in one screen

1. Both creators define a story as a change: a before state, one event that flips it, an after state. A calls the flip plot point one (PP1). B's skeleton is Identity, Conflict, Choice, Change. If you cannot name the flip, you do not have a story yet (A [6:48]).
2. A's minimum structure: a short setup, PP1, a build-up, PP2 at 75 to 85% of the runtime, a resolution. Reels need only PP1 (A [7:28]).
3. A's one hard rule: never open on PP1 unexplained, because the viewer must know what it changes; yet the setup must be very short, because free videos get skipped (A [4:25-4:57]).
4. B puts a hook in front of the skeleton and loops inside it: a new open question every 15 to 30 seconds (B [7:22]), with explicit why, how and what questions between them (B [7:50]).
5. B's five attention tools: open loops, pattern breaks, escalation, progress markers, stakes (B [8:39-16:08]). Progress markers (ordinal signposts that each promise the next reward) are the most scriptable device in either video.
6. B's hook formula: a real value plus an emotional root such as speed, feeling smart, or the underdog against a rival (B [17:56]). Two line templates: the X-Y rule and the information gap (B [19:31]).
7. Perspective (B [10:27], [21:52]): replace a label with a concrete habitual detail, and show a big number against something the viewer can picture.
8. Both videos bridge sections with a question in the viewer's voice and punctuate heavily with sound effects; B adds a sidekick who voices the viewer's doubts.
9. Weak spots: invented numbers (two plot points leave you 70% done), unfalsifiable claims that everything runs on one framework, a fake bonus used as a loop, crude humor, and the news held up as a model for stirring emotions. None of these belong in client scripts.
10. For South Asian viewers the videos show what lands: films and ads everyone shares, local money and geography in the stakes, informal address, English technical terms inside the local language, comedy and a sidekick. For Bangladesh, swap in local references and Bangla connectors, and keep crude jokes out.

## 1. Video A: AniThing, "I Learned Something that Made Storytelling EASY for Videos"

### 1.1 What it teaches (the method, in order)

1. **Purpose first.** A story exists to hold people's emotions, not to collect numbers; every view is a person with feelings [0:25-0:35].
2. **The minimum toolkit is two plot points.** Someone who knows nothing about storytelling needs only PP1 and PP2; he claims these two alone will turn around every video [0:38-1:04].
3. **Definitions.** A plot point is an action, incident, event or piece of information that pushes the whole story or video in a new direction. The plot is the entire storyline or concept; plot points are the anchors inside it [1:04-1:22]. PP1 is the moment that starts the main part of the story [1:28-1:38]. PP2 is a second big action or event that turns the mood again, near the end [2:49-2:57].
4. **Build between the anchors.** Once both plot points exist he says you are about 70% done: build up to PP1, then build up to PP2 [3:06-3:19]. His hand-drawn graph (visible in the video at about 5:06) shows a short setup rising to PP1 in roughly the first fifth, a wavy build-up with several rises and falls, PP2 late, and a rising end.
5. **Timing.** After PP1 comes a steady run of story beats, information and flow that lasts until 75 to 85% of the timeline and builds PP2 [~5:04].
6. **Any concept can hang on the two anchors** [3:27-4:23]. Worked example: a video concept about photographing people's emotions every day for 30 days. Setup: you are an extreme introvert. PP1: whatever pushes you to start (frustration, failure, a challenge, chasing views, someone who inspired you, a friend who forced you); you choose it. Build-up to PP2. PP2 options: you quit after being humiliated, you get unexpected praise, you discover something, you meet someone new, a brand notices you [4:13]. He stresses that the options are endless.
7. **Never open on PP1** [4:25-4:32]. Without the motivation, the change means nothing. An introvert who starts taking close-up portraits of strangers has to be set up first [4:32]; Spider-Man's lonely, insecure, failing boy has to exist before the spider bite matters [4:39-4:49].
8. **Keep the setup very short and fast** [4:49-4:57], because viewers do not pay for our videos. He adds a crude aside that people fast-forward past the setup even in videos they went looking for [~5:00].
9. **Big anchors, not many small ones.** Great films contain many small plot points that each bend the direction a little; creators need mainly the two big ones [~5:01-5:08, ~5:28].
10. **Diagnosis when no plot point appears** [6:48-7:28]: you probably do not have a story yet. The concept is immature because you do not know exactly what happens and what to show from start to end. Fix: write a rough sequence of what happens, one thing after another, with no big gaps; after that the two anchors are easy to find or create.
11. **Reels**: PP1 alone is enough for a good story, one action that changes things. PP2 is not needed [7:28-7:40].
12. **Boring topics** [7:40-8:00]: successful screenplays put extreme changes at these points. You do not need that extremity; you need a real change caused by the plot point.
13. **Weighting for social media** [8:00-8:15]: PP1 is the main deciding factor. PP2 is usually a small change, unless you are building an unusually complex story.
14. **Last resort** [8:15-8:37]: if you still cannot make simple things look big, create conflict, move emotions or turn the story on one point, go and watch the news for inspiration (half joke, half method: the news frames ordinary events as conflicts with turns).

### 1.2 Mechanics that can become checks or templates

- **A-M1 Plot point test.** A beat qualifies only if it is an action, incident, event or new information, and the expected direction after it differs from before it (goal, plan, mood or knowledge). Check: the writer can complete "before this beat the viewer expects X; after it they expect Y" with Y different from X.
- **A-M2 Order.** Setup, PP1, build-up, PP2, resolution. PP1 is never beat 1.
- **A-M3 Position.** PP2 starts between 75 and 85% of the runtime. PP1 comes early: the graph places it at about the first fifth, and his FX30 example lands at about 0:48 (the film's total length is not given). **[Ours]** default PP1 window: 10 to 25% of long-form runtime.
- **A-M4 Build-up shape.** Between PP1 and PP2 tension rises and falls several times (the graph's waves) instead of climbing in a straight line.
- **A-M5 Short form.** PP1 required, PP2 optional and usually small.
- **A-M6 Change, not extremity.** Every plot point must cause a visible change; the change can be small.
- **A-M7 Concept maturity gate.** Before drafting, list every event from start to end with no big gaps. If PP1 cannot be pointed to in that list, stop and rework the concept.
- **A-M8 Menus.** PP1 triggers: frustration, failure, a challenge, a target such as views, inspiration from a person, pressure from a friend. PP2 turns: humiliation and quitting, unexpected praise, a discovery, a new person, outside recognition.

Template (from A):

```
SETUP        short     who, what normal looks like, what is missing
PP1          early     one action, incident, event or reveal that changes direction
BUILD-UP               beats that alternate progress and setback, each adding story or information
PP2          75-85%    a second turn: failure, praise, discovery, a person, recognition
RESOLUTION             the new state and what it means
```

Ambient synths, a sub-bass hit at 0:00, a tension riser at 0:05, a comic sound at 0:35 on a jab about Instagram virality. The first 30 seconds are abstract; the concrete promise only arrives at [0:38-0:57].
- **Promise** [0:38-1:04]. He announces something bold: you need just two things. Impact thuds land on plot point one and plot point two, and a bass drop lands as he declares that this is enough [0:49-0:57]. Then the transformation claim.
- **Sections**: definitions [1:04-1:38]; own film with a clip [1:38-2:32]; a hit film [2:32-3:06]; the 70% claim and a teased graph (he says he will show it even if you say no) [3:06-3:27]; worked concept over street and station B-roll [3:27-4:25]; the rule against opening on PP1, Spider-Man, short setup [4:25-~5:00]; the 75 to 85% timing, The Matrix, Titanic, big versus small anchors [~5:00-5:24]; sponsor [~5:24-6:48]; diagnosis and fix [6:48-7:28]; two FAQ-style closers, Reels and boring topics [7:28-8:15]; punchline ending [8:15-8:37].

### 1.4 Weak, unproven or questionable advice in A

- **Two plot points leave you 70% done.** An invented number. Two turns without a character's want, stakes or causality still make a flat video (R3-R1, R3-R2). The anchors are a skeleton, not most of the work.
- **PP2 at 75 to 85%** is feature-film convention [Craft]: Field puts plot point II near the end of act two, and Save the Cat's break into three sits at page 85 of 110, about 77% (R3 section 3). Film audiences are captive; on YouTube most viewers are lost early, so the first minute and the middle need more design than a late turn. Useful as a position check, not as a retention strategy.
- **Never open on PP1** clashes with the cold-open and in-medias-res practice R3 recommends (R3-R3, template T1) and with B's own 3 Idiots point [B 13:55]. The defensible version: never let PP1 land before the viewer knows what it changes; a flash-forward tease of the turn or its result is fine if the setup then explains it.
- **A short setup** is right, but he gives no measure; our numbers must be calibrated.
- **Almost every example is a feature film.** The transfer to an 8-minute creator video is asserted, not shown, and his one creator example withholds its PP2.
- **Watching the news to learn how to make simple things look big and move emotions** is ethically risky for factual and client work; it invites sensationalism, fake stakes and melodrama (R3 section 7).
- **The opening paradox is never paid off**, and the concrete promise comes at about 0:50, late for a hook.
- **Crude humor** narrows the audience and would be off-brand for most clients.

## 2. Video B: HimanshuG, "Let's Break It: Why Some Videos Are So Addictive?"

### 2.1 What it teaches (the method, in order)

The organizing metaphor [0:14-0:26]: a story is a body built in layers. Skeleton (structure); muscles and fat (feeling: hook, loops, why-how-what, then a lower body made of attention tools); blood vessels (the hook for the first seconds); skin (perspective and imagination); and an optional fifth layer, makeup, which turns out to be a coupon.

1. **Story means change** in a character, object or scenario; the change is the story [0:49-1:01], and it is the one constant in any story [1:23]. Viewers only itch to know how the change happened: why the troubled villagers of Lagaan celebrate at the end, how Walter White became Heisenberg, how Jimmy McGill became Saul Goodman, how a character called Raju turned confusion into a solution (the film is unclear from the audio), why Kattappa killed Baahubali [1:04-1:20]. Storytelling is telling that change in a structured way [1:26-1:44].
2. **Skeleton: Identity, Conflict, Choice, Change** [1:52-1:58], shown on screen as the root framework of every story. He claims Apple, Pixar, startup pitches and viral YouTube all run on it [2:00].
   - Identity: who is this, right now.
   - Conflict: what stands against them.
   - Choice: the decision that sets the next goal toward change.
   - Change: the end state, different from the start.
3. **The value of content is the lesson the viewer takes from the change** [4:56-5:02].
4. **Muscles decide how it feels.** The skeleton says what happened; the muscles decide how it feels to the viewer [6:06]. A bare skeleton reads like the nursery line about a king and a queen who both died, the end [6:14-6:28].
5. **Add a hook in front** for social media: Hook, Identity, Conflict, Choice, Change [6:28-6:40]. He calls the hook the protein.
6. **Add loops inside** to hold viewers longer; he calls them the fat [6:45-6:53]. Without loops the story turns systematic and boring [7:16]. Thumb rule: a loop every 15 or 30 seconds, but your call [7:22-7:31]. Loops can even stand in for conflict and choice [7:31]. Long films keep you wanting more after three and a half hours because of loops [7:35].
7. **Why, how and what** questions placed between loops keep viewers, because each answer delivers value [7:41-8:03], illustrated with Simon Sinek's talk on Apple leading with why [8:08-8:33].
8. **Three itches come before the tools** [8:57-9:09]: curiosity, visuals, surprise.
   - **Curiosity itch**: created by an information gap and relieved when the gap closes [9:09-9:19]. He promises four techniques and names three: end things on an open loop (a countdown clip ends on a narrator line saying this beautiful story began somewhere else) [9:24-9:36]; withhold information [9:36]; add the word "but", as in an AI tool you trusted nearly wiping out your savings [9:39-9:50]. His last two examples are contrarian claims (five reasons a silver play button is useless; MrBeast supposedly hating views) [9:50-10:01], labelled on screen as open loops. The fourth technique is never named.
   - **Visual itch**: relieved by pattern breaks: a cut, a transition, a scene change, pushing focus onto an object, a visual metaphor [10:01-10:24]. Unless it is a podcast, a viewer who could shut their eyes and miss nothing is not being served.
   - **Perspective, first kind**: stories are told through perspective [10:27-10:54]. Do not say a man is poor; show that he counts coins every day before buying milk. A clip then opens by claiming the man on screen is India's biggest matchmaker and you have never heard of him [10:57].
   - **Pattern breaks inside this very video**: motion graphics between talking shots, his hand gestures, the alien sidekick suddenly appearing [11:03-11:19].
   - **Surprise itch**: surprises make a video unpredictable, and predictability wrecks watch time [11:24-11:35]. Methods: say the opposite of what is expected, then explain it (a Telugu word that is a slur in Hindi but an invitation in Telugu [11:41-11:54]; Surf Excel's claim that stains are good [11:55-12:07], which created surprise, curiosity, a wider gap and a brand identity at once); or a twist at the end (Game of Thrones' early seasons, which later grew predictable) [12:07-12:18].
9. **The framework is not a fixed order** [12:21-14:11]. A Hindi iPhone base-model review opens straight on a hook, skips identity and stays inside loops, promising that the camera's magic will show later [12:37-13:47]. Some stories show the ending first and walk back to the start, as 3 Idiots does in flashback [13:55-14:11]. Build your own framework, but the viewer must still see a change [14:11].
10. **The five attention tools** [14:14-16:08]. The sidekick asks what happened to the five; open loops and pattern breaks were tools 1 and 2 [14:17-14:21].
    - **Escalation**: make each attempt riskier and more dramatic; small becomes medium, medium becomes big. More risk means more curiosity, a bigger gap, more views. It does not always work; MrBeast uses it well [14:23-14:48].
    - **Progress markers**: like a map in a game, the viewer needs markers to keep walking toward the destination [14:48-15:03]. In a script, marker one names your first mistake and explains it; marker two promises a second thing nobody tells you; marker three promises that the biggest surprise is still ahead, so the viewer stays past the third marker and reaches the change [15:06-15:34]. On screen the script is drawn as bars with the three handwritten markers.
    - **Stakes**: creators raise their own stakes, for example promising to quit if they fail [15:39-15:49]. A travel clip: a creator holds up one lakh rupees he will donate after 30 days while trying to get from Lal Chowk in Srinagar to Kanyakumari within the month, under rules [15:49-16:00]. The creator has much to lose and the viewer much to gain, so the viewer stays [16:01-16:06].
11. The same devices work in titles, thumbnails and ad campaigns [16:15]; he adds that AI can do this only up to a point, because feeling, gut and instinct matter [16:21].
12. **Blood vessels: the hook** strengthens the whole structure and designs the first seconds [17:20-17:51]. **Hook framework: target a real value and hit an emotional root** [17:56]. The beginner line simply offers five ways to beat competitors. His three professional versions [18:07-18:58]:
    - Ask for one minute and promise that rivals will resent you: the value is quick, so the viewer thinks one minute is fine.
    - Five ways to beat the competition without them finding out: makes the viewer feel smart.
    - A named rival will hate me for this, followed by five unfair advantages (on screen with a blank for the competitor's name): the underdog emotion, plus a question about what could provoke such hate.
13. **Two templates he says always work** [19:20-20:42]:
    - **X-Y rule**: X is a problem or a curiosity, Y is its result, twist or promise [19:38]. Forms: if you are X, don't do Y (on-screen example: if you overthink, don't try to be disciplined) [19:53]; people do X, which is why they don't get Y [19:54]; you are growing followers, which is why sales aren't coming [19:58]. The rule creates an information gap by itself [20:01].
    - **Information gap**: reveal only as much as makes the viewer's brain ask what comes next [20:07]. The root formula is a single line, quoted because its exact wording is the device: "मैं वो जानता हूँ जो तुम नहीं जानते" ("I know something you don't know") [20:14]. He claims the whole economy turns on information gaps and that storytelling really means creating them; any element without a gap needs more work [20:29-20:42].
14. **Skin: perspective that makes viewers imagine** [21:16-23:36]. Telling is not enough; the fun of a story is in seeing [21:52-21:59]. Show perspective through shapes, illustrations or data [22:12]. Examples: their own MrBeast video animated the claimed 600 to 700 million dollars a year instead of printing a figure under a photo, so viewers feel the scale and where they stand [22:18-22:53]; KK Create went to Dharavi in person instead of describing it from a studio, so each viewer imagines it their own way [22:53-23:07]; a Prime drink ad with KSI and Logan Paul contrasts all the effort you would need with simply grabbing a can [23:10-23:32].
15. **Makeup** [23:40-23:53]: a promised bonus that the sidekick exposes as an open loop used to deliver a discount coupon (on screen: coupon code HOOKED).

### 2.2 Mechanics that can become checks or templates

- **B-M1 Beat roles.** Tag every beat HOOK, IDENTITY, CONFLICT, CHOICE, CHANGE, LOOP or VALUE. Required **[Ours, derived from B]**: HOOK first, at least one CONFLICT, one CHANGE stated as a before-and-after pair, one VALUE line (what the viewer takes away). IDENTITY and CHOICE may be skipped or reordered (B [13:43], [13:55]).
- **B-M2 Loop cadence.** A new open question, tease or unresolved beat at most every 15 to 30 seconds; measure the longest gap between loop openings.
- **B-M3 Why, how, what.** **[Ours, making B's advice testable]** Each major section answers one of the three explicitly. For persuasive segments follow Sinek's order: why, then how, then what.
- **B-M4 Information-gap moves.** A line counts as a gap if it ends a section on an unresolved question, withholds a named piece of information, pivots on "but", makes a contrarian claim, or says the speaker knows something the viewer does not.
- **B-M5 Pattern-break inventory.** Cut, transition, scene change, object focus, visual metaphor, motion graphic, gesture, character pop-in, black-screen beat. Check that the visual column changes type regularly and no single type runs long.
- **B-M6 Perspective rewrite.** Flag abstract labels (poor, rich, huge, a lot of money, very popular). Each needs a concrete habitual detail or a comparison the viewer can picture, in the same beat.
- **B-M7 Surprise beat.** At least one opposite-of-expected claim, explained within the next beat or two.
- **B-M8 Escalation ladder.** In attempt or challenge formats, each attempt raises the risk or size over the last one.
- **B-M9 Marker ladder.** Ordinal markers (first, second, biggest), each promising the next reward, the last framed as the biggest; spoken and shown on screen.
- **B-M10 Stakes pair.** Early in the script: what the creator or character can lose (concrete: money, time, a public promise) and what the viewer gains by staying.
- **B-M11 Hook equals value plus emotion.** The first line names a concrete value (a result, a number, a time) and hits one emotional root: speed or ease, feeling smart, underdog against a rival, fear of loss.
- **B-M12 Question bridges.** Each new section opens on a question in the viewer's voice, from a sidekick or the narrator.

Templates from B (abstracted; slots in brackets):

### 2.4 Weak, unproven or questionable advice in B

- **The claim that Apple, Pixar, pitches and viral videos all run on this framework** is unfalsifiable. The four steps are a useful simplification of the idea that change is the story (R3 lists Dicks and Kindra Hall), not a discovery.
- **A loop every 15 to 30 seconds** has no source [Craft, Unverified]. Loops that never pay train viewers to distrust promises, and the Zeigarnik memory claim fails replication (R3 2.3). Keep the cadence as a heuristic and pair it with a payoff ledger.
- **Loops standing in for conflict and choice** invites tease-bait: curiosity with nothing at stake.
- **Why, how, what** comes from brand messaging, not narrative, and the talk's brain-biology claims are contested. The instruction to put the three questions between loops is too vague to apply without a rule like B-M3.
- **Information gaps as the whole economy and the real meaning of storytelling**: overstated. Curiosity is well supported (R3 2.2), but emotion, character and stakes matter as much.
- **Escalation brings more views** is asserted without data (he admits it does not always work) and pushes toward stunt content.
- **Quit-if-I-fail stakes** are easy to fake; R3 warns against fake stakes (R3 section 7).
- **The hook examples lean on rivalry and resentment.** Fine for some creator niches, wrong for many brands; the beginner-versus-professional comparison is not tested.
- **The fake bonus** is self-aware, but still a broken promise. Never in client work.
- **The fourth information-gap technique is never named**, and the layers overlap (hooks sit in two layers, perspective in two, the itches duplicate the tools), which makes the method hard to apply as stated.
- **Offensive material**: the sperm opener and the Telugu slur joke will put off family audiences, brands and many Bangladeshi viewers.
- **The line that AI can do this only up to a point** comes just before a product pitch and is not an argument.
- **The idea that the brain imagines even what is in front of it in its own way** is vague pop psychology; the practical advice (show it, go there, make scale visible) stands without it.

## 3. Synthesis for our skills

### 3.1 Where the two videos agree, and the one conflict

Agreement:

- A story is a caused change. A: setup, PP1, build-up, PP2. B: identity, conflict, choice, change. Both reduce to before, turn, after.
- The setup is a cost to keep short, and the hook comes first.
- Section boundaries are bridged with a question in the viewer's voice (A [3:19], [~5:12], [7:28], [7:40]; B [5:53], [8:34], [14:14], [21:07]).
- The viewer should always know the next step. A's cold open says it (you are clear only when you know exactly the next step); B builds it with markers and the body map.

Conflict: A says never open on PP1; B shows hook-first and ending-first structures working. Resolution for nexa-script: the hook may show the result or tease the turn, but the turn itself must not land before the viewer knows what it changes. This agrees with R3-R3 and R3's short-form template T1.

### 3.2 Rules to adopt (SR, extending R3-R1 to R3-R12)

- **SR1 Change first.** Before drafting, write the before state, the after state and the one event that flips it. If you cannot, the concept is not ready: list the events from start to end with no gaps, then find PP1 (A-M7). Extends R3-R1.
- **SR2 Two anchors in long form.** Anything over about 3 minutes has PP1 early (default 10 to 25% of runtime) and PP2 at 75 to 85%. Short form needs only PP1; PP2 is an optional small twist.
- **SR3 Setup budget.** Only what the viewer needs to feel PP1: who, what normal looks like, what is missing. Default: 10% of long-form runtime, 3 seconds in short form **[Ours, calibrate on retention data]**.
- **SR4 Hook plus skeleton.** Every script follows Hook, Identity, Conflict, Choice, Change, Value. Identity and choice may be reordered or skipped; conflict and change may not.
- **SR5 Value line.** Make clear what the viewer takes from the change, as a shown result or one plain sentence (R3-R10 still bans a closing moral).
- **SR6 Loop cadence with payoffs.** Open a new question, tease or unresolved beat at least every 30 seconds in long form (B says 15 to 30), log each one in the loop ledger (R3-R4) and close all of them. Cross-video loops must be declared and kept to the ending.
- **SR7 Question bridges.** Open each section with the viewer's likely next question, voiced by the narrator or a character.
- **SR8 Keep the viewer oriented.** At every section start the viewer knows the next step, not the whole map and not nothing. In list and explainer scripts use ordinal markers that promise the next reward, the last framed as the biggest, mirrored on screen.
- **SR9 Honest stakes.** Early on, say concretely what the creator or character can lose (money, time, a public promise) and what the viewer gains. No invented stakes in factual or client work.
- **SR10 Escalation only where the format has attempts.** Each attempt bigger or riskier than the last.
- **SR11 One fair surprise.** At least one opposite-of-expected claim, explained right after it, and true.
- **SR12 Show scale and perspective.** Replace labels with habitual details, show big numbers against something the viewer knows, and prefer going to the place over describing it.
- **SR13 Plan pattern breaks in the visual column.** Every beat names its visual type; alternate types; plan inserts for long talking stretches.
- **SR14 Hook equals value plus emotional root.** Pick the root on purpose (speed or ease, feeling smart, underdog against a rival, fear of loss) and make sure the body pays it off.
- **SR15 Ads and CTAs sit inside a loop.** Place a sponsor or product pitch after a loop opens and before it pays off. Never carry a CTA on a fake bonus or fake loop.
- **SR16 No manufactured drama.** Borrow the news's framing skills (conflict, turn, consequence) only for real conflicts; no inflation and no emotional manipulation in client or factual work.

### 3.3 Templates (ST)

**ST1 Long-form story explainer, 6 to 15 minutes: two anchors plus loops** [Ours, built from A and B; runtime percentages]:

```
0-5%      HOOK: the result or the turn teased; value plus emotional root; first loop opens
5-12%     SETUP: identity (who, what normal is, what is missing); stakes pair stated
10-25%    PP1: one event, decision or reveal that changes direction
25-75%    BUILD-UP: conflict and choice beats. Each section opens on a question bridge,
          carries a marker and closes on an open question. A new loop at least every 30 s.
          One fair surprise. Escalation if the format has attempts.
75-85%    PP2: the second turn (failure, praise, discovery, a person, recognition, a reveal)
85-97%    CHANGE and VALUE: the after state shown, what the viewer takes away;
          all loops closed, the main one last
97-100%   CTA or one declared cross-video loop
```

**ST2 Short form, 20 to 60 seconds**: a hook line in the first 2 s (the result or the contradiction), minimum setup by 5 s, PP1 by about a third of the runtime, the change and the value, an optional small twist near the end, and a last line that closes the hook's loop (R3 T1).

**ST3 Marker ladder for list videos**: marker 1 (name the first mistake or step), payoff; marker 2 (the thing nobody tells you), payoff; marker 3 (the biggest surprise is still ahead), payoff; the change is reached. Every marker also appears as on-screen text.

### 3.5 What a judge should score (SJ, 1 to 5, extending R3-J1 to R3-J10)

| Criterion | 1 | 3 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| SJ1 Turning points | no clear turn | a turn exists but is weakly caused or does not change direction | PP1 (and PP2 in long form) clearly caused and felt; the direction really changes |
| SJ2 Setup economy | a long preamble before anything happens | the setup is clear but padded | only what the viewer needs to feel the turn |
| SJ3 Orientation | the viewer often cannot tell where this is going | a roadmap but few markers | the viewer always knows the next step; markers and bridges pay off |
| SJ4 Loop craft | no loops, or teases never paid | loops present but generic or unevenly spaced | specific loops at a steady cadence, every one paid, the main one last |
| SJ5 Perspective and scale | labels and bare numbers | some concrete details | labels replaced with things the viewer can see; big numbers made physical |
| SJ6 Escalation (only for attempt formats) | flat attempts | some rise | each attempt clearly bigger or riskier |
| SJ7 Honesty | fake stakes, a fake bonus or inflated claims | minor overstatement | every promise, stake and claim is true and paid |
| SJ8 Cultural fit | references, register or humor wrong for the audience | neutral | references, units, form of address and humor feel native to the audience in the brief |

R3's J2 Hook, J4 Stakes and J6 Surprise already cover the hook formula, the stakes pair and the surprise beat; the judge prompt should cite SR14, SR9 and SR11 as their anchors. As R3 says, the judge runs as a separate model and is never the only gate.

### 3.6 South Asian audiences (India and Bangladesh)

What the two videos show working with Hindi-speaking Indian viewers, and how it carries to Bangladesh:

- **Shared references as shorthand.** Both creators lean on films and ads their audience already knows (Laapataa Ladies, Chak De! India, Lagaan, 3 Idiots, Baahubali, Koi... Mil Gaya, Fevicol, Surf Excel). One reference replaces a paragraph of setup. Many Bangladeshi viewers know the big Hindi films, but Indian-only ad campaigns may not register, and local references (natok, cricket moments, well-known local brands and places) build more trust. Check every reference against the audience in the brief; apply none of this when the brief's audience is elsewhere.
- **Local money and geography in the stakes.** Rupees counted in lakh, a Srinagar to Kanyakumari run. For Bangladesh use taka with lakh and crore, and local routes (Teknaf to Tetulia is the natural end-to-end journey). Convert dollar figures and show them against a local scale (SR12).
- **Register.** Both creators use the informal Hindi "tum" and keep English technical terms (hook, loop, framework, value) inside the Hindi. For Bangladeshi scripts: everyday চলিত Bangla with English terms kept as they are; "আপনি" as the default address for broad and client work; "তুমি" only for a young niche that talks that way. Use Bangla connectors (কিন্তু, তবে), never Hindi ones (SL18).
- **On-screen text.** B's text cards are Roman-script Hinglish. For Bangladesh, test Bangla script against Banglish for the specific audience; default to Bangla script for broad audiences, with English terms in Latin script.
- **Comedy and a sidekick** keep South Asian creator explainers moving (B's alien, A's asides). A voiced audience surrogate would suit our Bangla explainers too. Sexual jokes, slurs and mockery of another region's language (B's Telugu joke) carry high risk with family audiences in both countries and never enter client scripts (SL17).
- **Free content, fast skipping.** A's reason for a short setup (people do not pay, so they skip) is sharpest for mobile viewers on metered data: the hook in the first seconds, the setup in a breath.
- **Speed.** Both narrators run at about 200 transcript tokens per minute of Hinglish. Do not reuse that figure for Bangla timing; calibrate words per minute for Bangla separately in nexa-speech and in `script.py timing`.
- **Themes that resonate** here: the underdog (a disgraced coach, a shy introvert), public shame and honor (humiliation is one of A's PP2 options), family and exam pressure, the jump from a small town to a big city. Use them when the story is true to them; do not manufacture them.

### 3.7 Do not adopt

- The 70% figure and the claim that the whole economy runs on information gaps, as stated facts.
- Fake bonuses and self-exposing fake loops in client scripts.
- Watching the news as a method for stirring emotions.
- Rivalry and resentment hooks as a default; offer them only when the brand voice allows.
- Crude openers and jokes about another region's language.
