# Voice: how people talk, and how to write like them

The craft behind `SKILL.md`, in English with notes that hold in every language. Research of 24 September 2026:
`~/.claude/skills/codex-design/references/research/voice-casual-en.md` (the craft, with 1,658 brand posts as a
baseline), `voice-evidence.md` (studies, surveys, platforms, ethics) and `voice-humanizer-skills.md` (74 humanizer and
anti-slop projects). Examples are written for this file unless a brand is named.

## 1. What makes copy feel human or machine-made to readers

Ranked by the strength of the evidence (studies, surveys, platform statements), strongest first.

| Reads machine-made | Reads human instead |
|---|---|
| Generic lines any brand could post; nothing checkable | a name, a place, a time, a real number, a sensory detail, a decision rule |
| Contrast frames: "it's not X, it's Y", "not just X but Y", "X isn't the problem. Y is." | say the thing directly |
| Cliches and stock phrases (cliche density tracks AI detection best, r = 0.60) | fresh, literal words tied to this product |
| AI-favoured words: delve, underscore, showcase, intricate, tapestry, palpable, vibrant, meticulous | everyday words |
| Even rhythm: every sentence mid-length (ChatGPT's length variation 0.50 against 0.78 for people) | a four-word line next to a thirty-word one |
| Over-explaining, padding, telling instead of showing | cut to the new information; trust the reader |
| The tidy, self-announcing shape: the payoff promised in the headline, a roadmap line, a closer that restates the point, escalated stakes, "the old way against the new way" (structure alone flags AI posts at 98 F1) | start with the thing; end on the last fact or the one ask |
| Triads and mechanical parallels | uneven lists; one strong item |
| Noun-heavy density: "the implementation of", trailing "-ing" clauses, "X and Y" pairs (GPT-4o: participial clauses 5.3x, nominalisations 2.1x the human rate) | verbs, and people doing things |
| Sameness across a feed: the same openers, skeletons and CTAs | vary the pattern, keep the voice |
| An impersonal, flawless voice: no "I" or "we", no contractions | someone talking, in the market's register |
| Relentless positivity with no friction | the problem, the trade-off, the annoying part |
| Safe copy with no surprise, humour or opinion | one real surprise: an analogy, a joke, a view |
| Awkward near-miss words and ornamental "purple" phrasing (the top edit professional writers make, 28 %) | plain, exact words |
| Invented quotes and personas; default names (Emily, Sarah in 63 to 70 % of model articles) | only real, attributed quotes in the speaker's words |
| Machine-sounding thanks, apologies, condolences, replies | written or rewritten by the person who signs |
| Punctuation tics: the em dash, emoji bullets, bold labels, colon titles, arrows | plain sentences with normal variety (brackets, questions, quoted speech) |
| Engagement bait, fake urgency, hype CTAs | a real reason, a real date or count |
| The wrong register for the channel: textbook, notice, translated | the market's own social register |
| No person or point of view behind it | a named person, a real experience, a stated view |

Blind, many readers prefer fluent AI copy; once they recognise it, they punish it. Heavy AI users recognise it almost
every time, even after "humanising" tools. The only lasting fix is copy that is true, specific and native.

## 2. What the most human voices do

| Team | Borrow this |
|---|---|
| Mailchimp | Tone follows the reader's mood; the voice stays. Clear beats entertaining. One "!" at most, never in failures. "Forced humor can be worse than none at all." |
| Nielsen Norman Group | Set four dials per channel: formal or casual, serious or funny, respectful or irreverent, matter-of-fact or enthusiastic. Casual and conversational won their test, but trust explained 52 % of willingness to recommend; a playful insurer read as less trustworthy. |
| GOV.UK, digital.gov | Plain English, one person to one person, "you", active voice, sentences under 25 words, a words-to-avoid list, no hidden verbs. Serious, not pompous. |
| Monzo | Latin-root business words (require, provide) sound cold. "We're sorry", never "We'd like to apologise". No passive in bad news. Wit: none in service and operations, some in marketing, most on social. No cheap seasonal puns. Never punch down. |
| Duolingo | Brief, active, contracted; celebrate wins, soften failures; drop the exuberance for serious stories. |
| Intercom | Answer like a person, own mistakes with the real context, emoji only in light moments, never forced. |
| 37signals | Speak the customer's language, not internal lingo. |
| Paul Graham | Ask of every sentence whether you would say it to a friend; if a draft is stiff, explain it aloud and write down what you said. |
| Innocent | The voice came from how the team talked; skip your first idea and your second (everyone has them). |
| Oatly, Liquid Death, Ryanair | Surprise plus honesty; answer critics with self-awareness; self-deprecation works only about small things your promise can carry. |
| Cautions | Wendy's posted a meme whose meaning had turned hateful; Netflix joked with viewers' data and was called creepy; Zomato dropped personal food nudges as manipulative; Swiggy's festival joke told a community how to celebrate. Speed needs a check; data jokes and festival policing are out. |

The common thread: one speaker talking to one reader, in words they would say aloud, noticing specifics, with the
rhythm of speech, answering the next question, sharing a joke rather than making one at the reader's cost, admitting
limits, and reading the room.

## 3. The moves, with before and after

1. **Talk to one person.** "Customers can collect orders after 5 pm" becomes "You can pick it up after 5."
2. **Contract, except where a misread costs something.** "We are open late on Fridays and it is worth the trip"
   becomes "We're open late on Fridays. Worth the trip." Keep "Do not give this to children under 12" in full. Never
   mix "can't" and "cannot" in one piece.
3. **Everyday words.**

   | Stiff | Everyday | Stiff | Everyday |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | utilise | use | in order to | to |
   | purchase | buy | prior to | before |
   | assist, assistance | help | subsequently | later, then |
   | approximately | about | in the event that | if |
   | commence | start | at this point in time | now |
   | facilitate | run, host (say how) | due to the fact that | because |
   | obtain | get | a number of | some, several |
   | require | need | on a daily basis | daily |
   | additional | more, extra | is able to | can |
   | numerous | many | regarding | about |
   | endeavour | try | we wish to inform you | (say the news) |
   | initiate, commence | start | going forward | from now on |
   | terminate | end, cancel | one-stop shop | everything in one place |
   | demonstrate | show | deliver value | (name the result) |
   | individuals | people | state-of-the-art | (the spec or the proof) |
   | whilst | while | we strive to, we pride ourselves on | (say what you do) |

4. **Verbs, not nouns.** "Our team conducted a thorough review and made the decision to implement changes" becomes
   "We checked every order from last week and changed how we pack glass."
5. **Active voice, above all in bad news.** "Mistakes were made and your order was delayed" becomes "We sent your
   order to the wrong depot. It's with you tomorrow by noon."
6. **Rhythm.** Average 15 to 20 words, with variety; And, But and So may start a sentence. "Our bread is baked fresh
   daily using traditional methods" becomes "We bake at 5. And yes, it's still warm at 8."
7. **Specifics a person would notice.** "Enjoy our cosy atmosphere" becomes "Window seats go first. Ask for Dan, he
   knows the playlist."
8. **Questions people can answer.** "Ready to transform your mornings?" becomes "Oat or whole milk? We've got both
   now." No "Did you know?", "Ever wondered?", "Picture this:".
9. **Reactions one at a time,** and only reacting to something real: "Honestly?", "Fair point.", "Yes, really." No
   "OMG" or "lol" in a post; in a reply only if the other person wrote that way first.
10. **One joke, shared, never on bad news.** Self-deprecation about small things only; no wit in outages, price
    rises, fraud, grief or complaints about loss.
11. **Self-aware and honest.** "It's heavier than it looks." "We don't do refills yet."
12. **Read it aloud.** If you wouldn't say it across a counter, rewrite it.
13. **Plain is not dull.** Conversational but serious bank copy read friendlier, more trustworthy and more
    recommendable than formal copy (NN/g).
14. **Write positively.** "You can't get a refund without a receipt" becomes "Bring your receipt and we'll refund you
    on the spot."

## 4. The register ladder

| Rung | Sounds like | Markers | "The 6 pm class moves rooms tonight" |
|---|---|---|---|
| Friend-chat | a regular in the comments | slang the audience uses, fragments, emoji, lower case in replies | "6 pm spin's in studio 2 tonight 🚲 same Maya, way more bikes" |
| Casual | a friendly shop owner | contractions, "you", everyday words, one light joke | "Tonight's 6 pm class is in studio 2. Same instructor, more bikes." |
| Neutral-warm | a good nurse or bank adviser | plain words, "you", facts first, no jokes or emoji, one sorry if at fault | "Tonight's 6 pm class will be in studio 2. The time and the instructor stay the same." |
| Formal | a clear notice | full forms, "must", "do not", no humour, still plain | "On 24 September the 6 pm class will take place in Studio 2. The timetable is otherwise unchanged." |

Defaults (posts / replies / bad news):

| Brand type | Posts | Replies | Bad news |
|---|---|---|---|
| Snacks, soft drinks, streetwear, gaming, youth apps | friend-chat or casual | friend-chat if invited, else casual | neutral-warm |
| Restaurants, cafes, food delivery, fashion retail | casual | casual | neutral-warm |
| Beauty and wellness | casual, no purple | casual | neutral-warm; health claims formal |
| Luxury and hospitality | neutral-warm: short, concrete, no slang, no purple | neutral-warm, first name | neutral-warm or formal |
| Banks and fintech | casual in marketing; neutral-warm in product and operations | neutral-warm | neutral-warm, no wit; legal formal |
| Health, pharma, insurance | neutral-warm | neutral-warm | formal for dosage and safety |
| B2B software | casual on social; neutral-warm in docs and status pages | neutral-warm | neutral-warm; security notices formal |
| Government, NGOs, charities | neutral-warm ("serious but not pompous") | neutral-warm | formal and quiet |

Moving on the ladder: stakes set the rung (drop one as the reader's risk rises); never climb in bad news; an
unfamiliar brand starts one rung lower (a human voice helps browsing readers and can backfire with worried ones);
match the person one rung calmer; only an "exciting" brand can roast, and only when asked; a festival goes one rung
warmer with no offer on solemn days; a tragedy goes formal and quiet; on a celebrity death, a court case or a
political fight, say nothing.

## 5. What casual does not mean

| Failure | What it looks like | Why it fails |
|---|---|---|
| Forced slang | "No cap, this deal slaps." | 74 % of US adults say brands try too hard to be cool; negative reactions to slang outnumber positive 26 % to 15 % (CivicScience 2026) |
| Borrowed dialect | copy in a community's dialect it does not belong to | taking a community's voice to sell |
| Dated slang | on fleek, bae, totes, amazeballs | shows the brand arrived late |
| Misread meme or hashtag | a meme whose meaning changed; a trending tag about pain | you inherit what it means now |
| Shock hook | a cruel first line "explained" in the thread | nobody reads the thread |
| Jolly post on a solemn day | a mascot on a day of loss | grief used as content |
| Creepy data joke | "We see you ordered pizza four times this week" | reads as surveillance |
| Over-familiarity | "Hey bestie", "we love you", "our amazing community" | strangers don't want a brand as a friend |
| Sarcasm at customers | "Skill issue." | observers side with the customer |
| Jokes over a real grievance | cute trend posts while people are angry | reads as dodging |
| Cheap seasonal puns | eggcellent, sleigh, spooktacular | everyone posts the same pun that day |
| Confirmshaming, clingy win-backs | "No thanks, I don't like saving money." "We miss you! Don't leave us hanging." | guilt instead of news; a deceptive pattern |

Stay neutral-warm or formal for money trouble, fraud and security, health and dosage, grief, disasters and
anniversaries of tragedies, outages, recalls, price rises and layoffs, legal terms and complaints about loss.

## 6. Purple prose: the list and the plain version

Purple prose draws attention to its own style: stacked adjectives, adverbs and metaphors. Test: could a reader check
it, taste it, count it or find it? If not, replace it with something they could.

| Purple | Plain | Purple | Plain |
|---|---|---|---|
| a symphony (dance, burst) of flavours | name the flavours in order | dances across your palate | sweet first, then a chilli kick |
| tantalise your taste buds | say what it tastes like | delectable, heavenly, divine | good, or the dish |
| culinary journey, gastronomic adventure | meal, seven courses | whispers of vanilla | a hint of vanilla |
| timeless elegance | the same cut since 2009 | captivating, mesmerising, enchanting | name what holds the eye |
| exquisite | hand-rolled edges, 18k gold | ethereal | light, sheer, pale |
| thoughtfully designed, expertly crafted, lovingly made | hand-stitched in Porto | effortlessly chic | goes with jeans |
| a sanctuary, a haven, an oasis of calm | a quiet room, no phones past the desk | tranquil, serene, idyllic | quiet, or describe the view |
| kissed by golden sunlight | sun until 4 pm | awaits you, beckons | opens at 6 |
| immerse yourself in, step into a world of | spend an afternoon in | your next escape | trip, stay |
| the essence (soul, spirit) of summer | one smell, one street, one dish | feed your soul | hot, filling, ready in ten minutes |
| unforgettable, cherished memories | what they'll remember | pure bliss | calm, happy |
| a celebration of, an ode to, a love letter to | our take on | a new chapter | what changed |
| unparalleled, unrivalled, like no other | the proof | iconic | on the menu since 1978 |
| opulent, sumptuous, lavish | the material, the price | magical | what happens |

Before and after: "Indulge in a symphony of flavours that dance across your palate" becomes "Sour cherry first, then
dark chocolate. A 70 % bar with sea salt." "Step into a sanctuary where time slows down" becomes "No phones past the
front desk. Twelve rooms, one garden."

## 7. What a 2026 model writes by default: templates

A 2026 frontier model asked for captions wrote almost no purple words, but 50 of 72 captions used a launch template;
real brand posts did in 2.3 % of cases. Each template is fine once; the machine sign is reaching for one every time.

- "is here", "has arrived", "just landed", "just dropped"
- "Meet our", "Say hello to", "Introducing"
- "Discover our", "Explore our"
- a comparative with nothing compared: "a quieter moment", "a softer finish"
- a personified object: "Your mug has been waiting for this", "Your sneaker rotation noticed"
- meme casual: "Autumn called", "has entered the chat", "Cue the", "Finally, a way to", "just got easier"
- "New season, new menu"; "X is calling"; "the warmth of"; "X meets Y."
- social formulas from the humanizer projects: "Meet X, your new favourite", "Think X meets Y", "Thank me later",
  "Save this for later", a closing "What do you think?", "Pro tip:", "*chef's kiss*", "(yes, really)", the self-Q&A
  volley ("Is it worth it? Yes.")

The lint warns when two templates share one piece; the ledger catches one template in 3 of a client's last 12 posts.
Rewrite one of them around a fact.

## 8. Writing for the ear (reels, ads, explainers, host reads)

1. Write it the way you'd say it, then act it out with someone. Use contractions.
2. One breath, one idea (about 8 to 14 words).
3. Main point first; don't make the listener hold "Although..., because..., if..." before the verb.
4. Say numbers the way people say them; round what you can; one number per sentence.

   | Written | Said |
   |---|---|
   | $19.99 | nineteen ninety-nine |
   | £12.50 | twelve fifty |
   | ৳১,৫০০ | দেড় হাজার টাকা, or এক হাজার পাঁচশো টাকা |
   | 2nd | second |
   | 9:30 pm | half past nine tonight |
   | 3x faster | three times faster |
   | 50% off | half price |
   | shop.com/deals | shop dot com (the rest goes in the caption) |
   | e.g., i.e., vs. | for example, that is, versus |

5. No brackets, asides, "the former" or "the latter"; no visual pointers ("see below") in audio-only copy.
6. Lists of three at most in one sentence.
7. Say the brand and the one action twice: early, and again at the end.
8. Pace: about 2.5 words a second. 15 seconds holds 35 to 40 words, 30 seconds 70 to 80, 60 seconds 140 to 160; plan
   10 % fewer for music and pauses.
9. For text-to-speech, render and listen: homographs (read, live, record) come out wrong. A pause is a full stop or a
   new line, never a dash.
10. Host reads are talking points, not scripts: give the facts and one claim, let the host speak from real experience
    only, and disclose the paid partnership.

`copylint --role voiceover` (or `--platform voiceover`) checks symbols, brackets, links, emoji, abbreviations, the
taka sign, currency signs, ordinals, homographs and sentences over 16 words.

## 9. Replies and community posts

What the evidence says: a conversational human voice builds trust; observers read your replies to other people and
reward humour that laughs with people; emoji in complaint replies cost professionalism unless the voice is already
human; 51 % of public complainers expect a public acknowledgement before a move to DM; a one-word reply reads cold.

1. Answer first, in their words. No opener script ("Thank you for reaching out", "We understand your frustration").
2. Use their first name; sign with a first name where the platform allows.
3. Say what happens next, who does it and by when.
4. One apology, owned, then the fix; explain instead of apologising when you did nothing wrong.
5. Acknowledge in public, move private details to DM; never ask for passwords or full card numbers.
6. Match their energy, one rung calmer: praise can get a joke back, a complaint gets facts.
7. No emoji in complaint replies.
8. Never the same reply three times in a thread.
9. Roasts only when invited.
10. In other people's threads, add something or stay out; no sales pitch in a creator's comments.
11. Community posts sound like a host: questions people can answer, members named with permission, what you changed
    because of their feedback.
12. Some threads get no joke at all: trials, deaths, disasters, anger about a real decision.

| Situation | Robotic | Human |
|---|---|---|
| Late delivery | "We apologize for any inconvenience caused. Your concern has been escalated." | "Sorry, Priya, 50 minutes is too long for a burger. I've refunded the delivery fee; it shows in 3 to 5 days. Sam" |
| Price rise question | "Please be advised that prices are subject to change." | "Yes, the monthly plan goes up to £9 on 1 November. Yours stays at £7 until your renewal on 3 December." |
| Praise | "Thank you for your valuable feedback!" | "This made our morning. Telling the kitchen right now." |
| A customer's joke | "We are pleased that you enjoyed our products." | "We accept no responsibility. Also, the almond one's back Saturday." |
| Outage | "Oops, our bad! Perfect excuse to touch grass." | "The app's down for payments right now. Cards still work in store. Next update here at 3:30." |
| Bereavement | "Dear valued customer, kindly submit the required documentation." | "I'm very sorry about your father. You can close his account by post or at any branch. I've sent you the one form you need." |

## 10. Signals you can measure (register notes, not proof of AI)

| Signal | Casual target | Worth a look at |
|---|---|---|
| Contractions (English) | most pairs contracted | two or more uncontracted and none contracted |
| Sentence length | mean 15 to 20 words | a mean over 25, any sentence over 35 |
| Reading ease (Flesch) | 60 or more | under 50; under 30 is hard |
| Syllables per word | about 1.5 | over 1.75, or over 22 % of words with three or more |
| Passive sentences | under 1 in 4 | more than 1 in 4 |
| Nominalisations | few | 6 or more per 100 words |
| -ly adverbs | few | over 5 per 100 words |
| Brochure flatness | a "you", a contraction or a number | 40 or more words with none of the three |
| Sentence-length variation | varied | under 0.5 across 8 or more sentences |
| Spoken pace | 2.5 words a second or less | over 2.7 |

Up to a fifth of real brand posts miss one of these; treat them as prompts to reread, not verdicts.
