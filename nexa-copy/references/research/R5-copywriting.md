# R5. Conversion copywriting: ads, social posts and landing pages (2024 to 2026)

Research file for the copy skill. Compiled 2026-09-26. Scope: paid ads (Meta, Google, TikTok, LinkedIn, YouTube), organic posts and captions (Instagram, Facebook, LinkedIn, X, Threads, TikTok, YouTube) and landing pages, grounded in the classics.

How to read it: [S#] points to the source list (publisher, title, URL, date). Untagged claims were checked on the primary or publisher page. "Secondary" marks a third-party summary or vendor dataset, "unverified" something I could not confirm here, "heuristic" our own working rule. Vendor social studies are large but correlational: read them as direction, not cause. Platform limits change often; the tables show official pages as read on 2026-09-26.

## 1. The ten findings that matter most

1. The reader's awareness decides the first line; the market's sophistication decides the claim [S5][S6].
2. The best lines are found in customers' own words, not invented [S10][S18].
3. Specific claims are believed; generalities and superlatives are discounted [S1][S2].
4. The opening does most of the selling: headline, first line before "more", first three seconds [S4][S42].
5. Plain, objective copy beats promotional copy in usability and conversion data [S27][S29].
6. The real limit is the visible fold, not the maximum [S30][S36][S38][S40].
7. Proof is regulated: fake or AI-invented reviews, hidden incentives and fake countdowns carry US and UK penalties, and the EU bans generic green claims from 27 September 2026 [S54][S60][S64].
8. Reviews lift sales, but perfect scores look fake: purchase likelihood peaks at 4.0 to 4.7 stars [S65].
9. Most ideas lose in tests, and small accounts can only detect big differences [S67][S68].
10. Hype and AI tells cost trust; naming "artificial intelligence" as the benefit even lowered purchase intent [S70][S72].

## 2. The classics: what still holds

### 2.1 Eugene Schwartz, Breakthrough Advertising (1966)

Premise: copy cannot create desire; it channels desire that already exists toward a product (from the book's opening chapter; unverified here). Two scales.

**Awareness**, what this reader knows [S5]. The traffic column is heuristic.

| Level | Reader state | The first line must | Typical traffic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unaware | Does not see the problem | Open with a story, a surprising fact or an identity; do not lead with the product | Cold video feeds |
| Problem aware | Feels the pain, knows no fix | Name the pain in their words, then show it is solvable | Cold or warm social |
| Solution aware | Knows fixes exist, has not chosen | Show your mechanism and why other routes fall short | Non-brand search, comparisons |
| Product aware | Knows you, not convinced | Answer the main objection with proof | Retargeting, review sites |
| Most aware | Ready, waiting for the right deal | Lead with the offer and terms | Email, cart, brand search |

**Sophistication**, how many similar claims the market has heard [S6]: stage 1, state the claim simply; 2, enlarge it; 3, introduce a mechanism (how it works); 4, improve the mechanism (faster, easier); 5, identification (the reader buys who they become). Crowded ad categories mostly sit at stages 3 to 5 (heuristic), so "better, faster" alone fails and a named mechanism or hard proof is needed.

### 2.2 Claude Hopkins, Scientific Advertising (1923) [S1]

Advertising is salesmanship in print, judged by traced returns (keyed coupons), not opinion. The chapter "Being Specific" still makes the best case for concrete copy: "Platitudes and generalities roll off the human understanding like water from a duck." Superlatives make readers discount everything; a definite figure implies tests. His examples: three and one-third times the light instead of more light; a retailer that swapped its lowest-price boast for its 3 per cent net profit; a 78-second shave; a brewer who described filtered air, bottles washed four times and 1,018 yeast experiments while rivals said pure. The brewer is the pre-emptive claim: be first to explain a process everyone uses. Aged: readers now assume exaggeration, so specifics need visible proof beside them.

### 2.3 David Ogilvy [S4]

"five times as many people read the headlines as read the body copy" (Confessions of an Advertising Man, 1963; secondary). Today the headline is the first line before "See more", the first three seconds of a video, RSA headline 1 and the thumbnail text.

### 2.4 John Caples, Tested Advertising Methods (1932, later editions) [S2]

Caples saw one mail-order ad sell 19 1/2 times as much as another for the same product, in the same space and publication; the difference was the appeal. His rules: what you say matters more than how you say it; the headline is the most important element; the best headlines offer self-interest or news, and curiosity works only when tied to self-interest; long headlines that say something beat short ones that say nothing; specifics beat generalities. "Do you make these mistakes in English?" beat a headline asking whether readers feared making mistakes: useful information beat a named fear. Lesson: test the angle before the wording.

### 2.5 Joseph Sugarman, The Adweek Copywriting Handbook [S3]

Every element of an ad exists to get the first sentence read, and the first sentence exists to get the second one read. His "slippery slide" keeps readers moving with "seeds of curiosity" that pull into the next paragraph. In posts and scripts this is literal: line 1 earns line 2, second 1 earns second 3.

### 2.6 Gary Halbert, The Boron Letters (1980s) [S7]

Twenty-five letters to his son, written from Boron Federal Prison Camp. The "starving crowd": a market that already wants what you sell beats a better product or better copy (secondary). Under broad targeting (Advantage+, Performance Max, TikTok automatic targeting), a precise callout such as "for landlords with 1 to 5 units" does part of the targeting (heuristic).

### 2.7 Robert Cialdini, Influence (1984; New and Expanded edition 2021, secondary)

| Principle | Honest use in copy | Abuse the skill must refuse |
|---|---|---|
| Reciprocity | A real free tool, sample or guide | "Free" that hides a paid commitment |
| Commitment | A small first step (quiz, free plan) | Pre-ticked boxes, trick consent |
| Social proof | Real counts, reviews, named customers | Fake or bought reviews, fake followers |
| Authority | Real credentials, test data | "Experts recommend" with no evidence |
| Liking | Real founder voice, real similarity | Fake personas, hidden paid endorsers |
| Scarcity | Real stock and real deadlines | Timers that reset, a false "2 left" |
| Unity (added 2016) [S9] | A shared identity that is true | A manufactured tribe or false affiliation |

Ethics line: Cialdini calls it "ethically wrong to trick or trap others into assent" and says urgency or exclusivity offers must be genuine [S8]; with unity, point out a real connection and never counterfeit one [S9]. Skill rule: a lever may only amplify a fact the reader could verify, and the copy must survive the reader learning everything behind it.

## 3. Modern conversion copywriting

**Joanna Wiebe and Copyhackers.** Research comes first. Review mining (2014) [S10]: read reviews of products your prospects already buy, not only competitors'; log pains, desired outcomes and verbatim phrases; lift them into headlines. Copyhackers reports that a rehab center's review-mined headline, "If you think you need rehab, you do", won 400% more clicks and 20% more form submissions than the control (single case, few test details). Wiebe's message map sorts findings into product facts, persona, motivators, problems and conversion factors [S12]; Nikki Elbaz tags interview notes by objection, motivation, unexpected benefit, emotional language and use case, and keeps master lists of sticky phrases [S13]. "Sticky" here means memorable customer wording worth reusing verbatim. A physical sticky-note sort (one message per note, clustered and ranked) is a common workshop method, but I found no Copyhackers page defining it (unverified).

**CXL** (unverified; cxl.com blocked automated reading). From memory: research before testing, a points-based test prioritization model (PXL) that rewards noticeable, data-backed changes, and clarity before cleverness in value propositions.

**Eddie Shleyner, VeryGoodCopy** [S22]: weekly micro-lessons since 2014 (about 95,000 subscribers, per his site), rooted in Ogilvy, Gary Bencivenga and Drayton Bird. A model of one idea per piece, told through a small story.

**Harry Dry, Marketing Examples** [S15][S16][S17][S18]: cut hard; write the way you talk; skip cliches such as unlock and empower; open an idea with the fact, not an adjective; write to one reader and name who it is for; make the button name the result instead of a generic sign-up; use customers' words. Design Academy asked each new sign-up "what is your biggest frustration with design?" and wrote its copy from the answers. Three tests often credited to Dry (can I visualise it, can I falsify it, could nobody else say it) were not found on his site (unverified), but they make strong judge questions.

**April Dunford** [S19]: positioning in dependency order: competitive alternatives, unique attributes, the value they enable, best-fit customers, market category (Obviously Awesome, 2019). Every claim is implicitly compared with the reader's alternative (spreadsheets, an agency, doing nothing), so name it.

**Julian Shapiro** [S20]: purchase rate is desire minus (labor plus confusion). The header says what you sell, not a slogan like "Supercharge your workflow"; the subheader says how it works and why the bold claim is believable; each feature block pairs a blunt value header with an objection-handling paragraph and a product image; the CTA continues the hero's promise.

**Ann Handley** [S21]: Everybody Writes (2nd edition, Wiley, October 2022) puts reader empathy at the center of business writing, with a step-by-step process and templates for 19 content types.

**Data on plain copy.**
- Nielsen Norman Group, 1997 [S27]: the same web text made concise tested 58% better in usability, scannable 47%, objective (no marketese) 27%, all three 124%; promotional claims made users doubt credibility.
- NN/g, 2008 [S28]: visitors read at most 28% of a page's words, 20% more likely.
- Unbounce 2024 [S29]: 41,000+ landing pages and 57M conversions, median conversion rate 6.6%; pages at a 5th to 7th grade reading level converted at 11.1%, 56% above 8th to 9th grade pages and about double professional-level pages (5.3%); word count correlated negatively. Correlation, not cause.
- Length follows questions: Conversion Rate Experts made a Crazy Egg page about 20 times longer, built on four researched objections, and measured a 30% lift [S26].

## 4. Research before writing

1. **Job.** The progress a person wants in a specific circumstance, with functional, social and emotional sides (Christensen and colleagues, HBR 2016) [S24] (secondary, paywalled).
2. **Forces** [S23]: the push of the current situation, the pull of the new solution, anxiety about the new, the habit of the present. Weak copy only adds pull; strong copy also names the push and shrinks anxiety and habit (setup time, risk, switching cost).
3. **Voice of customer**, in order of value: customer and lost-deal interviews; reviews of you, competitors and adjacent products (Amazon, G2, Capterra, Trustpilot, app stores); Reddit and niche forums; support tickets, sales calls and cancellation reasons; post-purchase polls, heatmaps and competitor audits [S11].
4. **Log it**, one row per verbatim quote:

| Quote (verbatim) | Source link | Segment | Awareness | Type | Intensity 1 to 3 | Count | Proof we hold |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Type values: pain, desired outcome, trigger, alternative, objection, anxiety, result.

5. **Interview prompts** (switch-interview style): When did you first look for something like this, and what happened that day? What were you using before, and what made it stop being good enough? What else did you consider? What nearly stopped you? What did you expect, and what happened? How would you describe it to a friend?
6. **Objection checklist:** price or value; will it work for me; effort to switch; trust in the company; risk and refunds; compatibility; I can do it myself; timing.
7. **Pick the angle:** the most frequent and most intense pain or desire that you can prove.

## 5. Frameworks as fill-in templates

Frameworks order ideas; they do not create them. AIDA traces to E. St. Elmo Lewis (around 1898 to 1910), and reviews of hierarchy-of-effects models found little empirical support for a fixed sequence [S25], so treat all of these as checklists, not laws. Letters follow the Copyhackers compendium [S14]; QUEST and PASTOR are usually credited to Michel Fortin and Ray Edwards (unverified).

- **AIDA.** Attention: [the reader's situation in one line]. Interest: [the fact that makes it real]. Desire: [the outcome in concrete terms, plus proof]. Action: [one CTA and what happens next].
- **PAS.** Problem: [the pain in their words]. Agitate: [the specific cost of leaving it; facts, not fear]. Solution: [product, how it works, proof].
- **BAB.** Before: [their day now]. After: [the same day with it solved]. Bridge: [how the product gets them there].
- **4Ps.** Picture: [a scene]. Promise: [a specific outcome]. Prove: [data, testimonial, demo]. Push: [CTA; urgency only if true].
- **FAB.** Feature: [what it is]. Advantage: [why it beats the alternative]. Benefit: [what the reader gets].
- **QUEST.** Qualify: [who it is for and not for]. Understand: [show you know their situation]. Educate: [the mechanism or new idea]. Stimulate: [outcomes and proof]. Transition: [to the offer].
- **PASTOR.** Problem, Amplify, Story (the solution), Transformation or Testimony, Offer, Response.
- **One reader, one idea, one action.** Write to one named reader [S16]; build the piece on one idea you can state in a sentence without "and" (the Agora "Rule of One", credited to Michael Masterson and John Forde, unverified); ask for one action.

| Awareness | Frameworks that fit | Proof load | Typical placements |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unaware | Story, BAB, curiosity tied to self-interest | Light early, strong at the end | TikTok, Reels, Shorts |
| Problem aware | PAS, BAB, AIDA | Medium | Meta feed, LinkedIn, organic |
| Solution aware | 4Ps, QUEST, AIDA | High: comparisons, demos | Non-brand search, landing pages |
| Product aware | FAB, 4Ps heavy on Prove, objection FAQ | Highest: reviews, guarantees, data | Retargeting, pricing pages |
| Most aware | Offer first | Price, terms, deadline | Email, brand search, promos |

The mapping is heuristic, built on [S5][S6] and the formulas above.

### 6.3 What the platforms advise

- **Google Search** [S30][S31]: supply as many unique headlines and descriptions as you can, in varied lengths; include keywords; put prices, promotions or exclusives in the text; use specific CTAs; pin only when a message must always show; run at least two ads rated Good or Excellent per ad group. Google's own figures: Ad Strength from Poor to Excellent brings 15% more clicks and conversions on average; a second RSA adds 6.6% conversions, a third 3.7%.
- **YouTube** [S34][S35]: the ABCD checklist (Attention, Branding, Connection, Direction): hook fast, brand early, one message, ask for the action in voice and text. A Google and Kantar study (April 2021) linked ABCD-following ads to 30% higher short-term sales likelihood and 17% higher long-term brand contribution. Shorts: under 60 seconds (10 to 30 for action goals), sound on (music or voice-over tied to 20%+ more conversions), creator-style footage.
- **TikTok** [S42]: hook, body, close. TikTok's own studies (2020 to 2022) report that 90% of ad recall impact happens in the first six seconds, that TikTok-first ads with native features and text overlays caught the attention of 74% of viewers, and that CTA cards lifted recall 45%.
- **LinkedIn** [S38][S43]: intro under 150 characters, headline under 70, legally required text in the intro. The LinkedIn B2B Institute, with the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, estimates only about 5% of B2B buyers are in market at any time, so most LinkedIn ads must build memory for later, not only ask for a demo.
- **Meta** [S36]: Feed primary text within 50 to 150 characters, headline near 27. Working rule: the whole sale lands in the first 125 or so characters (heuristic).

### 6.4 Caption and hashtag evidence (organic)

- Instagram capped hashtags at five per post and Reel (reported December 18, 2025); Adam Mosseri says hashtags label content but do not add reach [S47].
- Metricool 2026 (24.4M posts, 375,118 accounts, published June 16, 2026) [S48]: posts with at least one hashtag averaged 31.7% fewer views and 33.9% fewer interactions than the platform average (correlational); posts asking a question got 36.7% more comments; comment-focused CTAs got 202.8% more comments.
- Socialinsider (9.1M posts from 82,952 business accounts, January to July 2023, published February 21, 2024) [S49]: captions under 30 words had the highest engagement rate.
- Buffer (52M+ posts, March 5, 2026) [S50]: replying to comments went with higher engagement within the same account (Threads +42%, LinkedIn +30%, Instagram +21%, Facebook +9.5%, X +8%); LinkedIn document carousels had a 21.77% median engagement rate against 3.18% for text posts; on X, text posts (3.56%) beat link posts (2.25%).
- LinkedIn length: datasets disagree. AuthoredUp (372,126 posts, September 2025 to February 2026) found 1,301 to 2,500 characters engaged best [S51]; summaries of Richard van der Blom's 2025 report stress fewer, sharper posts amid falling reach [S52] (secondary). Fight for the first 140 characters and let length follow substance.

## 7. Proof and specificity

**Specificity ladder.** Level 0, vague: fast payroll. Level 1, specific: payroll in 10 minutes. Level 2, verifiable: median run time 9 minutes across 2,140 payrolls in June 2026. Level 3, attributed: a named owner, business and quote. Push every key claim to level 1, and to level 2 or 3 when the claim ledger allows. State unit, period, base (n) and source, and put the proof next to the claim. Odd, precise figures read as measured, as with Hopkins' three and one-third [S1].

**Reviews** (Spiegel Research Center, 2017) [S65]: showing five reviews raised purchase likelihood 270% against none (190% for cheaper products, 380% for pricier ones); likelihood peaked at 4.0 to 4.7 stars and fell toward 5.0; verified-buyer badges added 15%. Show real rating distributions; do not hide the negatives.

**Legal side** (not legal advice; route health, money, environmental and comparative claims to a human):
- **US, FTC rule on consumer reviews and testimonials** (16 CFR Part 465), announced August 14, 2024, effective October 21, 2024 [S54][S55]: bans fake reviews and testimonials, including AI-generated ones, that misstate real experience; paying for reviews conditioned on sentiment; undisclosed insider reviews; company-run "independent" review sites; review suppression; buying or selling fake followers or views. Civil penalties apply to knowing violations. Incentives for honest reviews stay legal if not tied to sentiment.
- **US, Endorsement Guides** (updated June 29, 2023) [S56][S57]: a testimonial with exceptional results needs proof that it is typical or a clear statement of what people generally get; a results-not-typical line alone is not enough; material connections must be disclosed clearly and early in a post; platform disclosure tools may not suffice.
- **US, substantiation and dark patterns**: hold a reasonable basis before the ad runs, and claims such as tests show or doctors recommend need that exact level of evidence (1984 policy) [S58]; the FTC's dark patterns report (September 15, 2022) names fake countdown timers and false limited-time claims as deceptive [S59].
- **UK, DMCC Act 2024, Schedule 20, paragraph 13** [S60][S61]: bans fake reviews, concealed incentivised reviews and false or misleading review information, and commissioning them; in force since April 2025; publishers must take reasonable and proportionate steps to prevent and remove them (CMA208, April 4, 2025). The CMA can now fine directly [S61]; a cap of 10% of global turnover is widely reported but unverified here. Wider guidance: CMA207 [S62].
- **UK, CAP Code** [S63]: hold documentary evidence for objective claims before publishing (rule 3.7); do not mislead (3.1) or exaggerate (3.11); specific rules cover "free" (3.23 to 3.26) and availability and limited-time claims (3.27 to 3.31).
- **EU, Directive 2024/825**, applied from September 27, 2026 [S64]: bans generic environmental claims (eco-friendly, green, climate friendly) without recognized excellent performance, climate-neutral claims based on offsets, uncertified sustainability labels, whole-product claims when only a part qualifies, and presenting a legal requirement as a distinctive feature.

## 8. Testing

Why: Caples saw a 19 1/2x gap from appeal alone [S2]; only a third of ideas tested at Microsoft improved their target metric, and about 10% of Google's experiments led to business changes [S67]; a Bing ad-headline display change shelved for months raised revenue 12%, over $100M a year in the US [S66].

What to test first, big levers first (heuristic, drawn from [S2][S16][S20]): (1) angle or appeal; (2) offer (trial, guarantee, bonus, price framing); (3) hook or headline; (4) proof type (number, testimonial, demo); (5) format (video, carousel, static); (6) CTA wording; (7) length. Synonyms and button colors come last.

Methods: platform tools (Meta Ads Manager has an A/B test tool [S69]; Google Ads experiments and RSA asset reports, unverified here); landing-page splits with a fixed sample; cheap early signal from organic posts or small-budget ads before scaling (unverified practitioner method); holdout groups where possible.

**Sample size per variant**, visitors (conversions), two-sided alpha 0.05, 80% power, computed for this file with the standard two-proportion formula:

| Baseline rate | Detect +10% | +20% | +30% | +50% |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1% | 163,095 (1,631) | 42,693 (427) | 19,827 (198) | 7,750 (78) |
| 3% | 53,211 (1,596) | 13,914 (417) | 6,455 (194) | 2,518 (76) |
| 5% | 31,234 (1,562) | 8,158 (408) | 3,780 (189) | 1,471 (74) |
| 10% | 14,751 (1,475) | 3,841 (384) | 1,774 (177) | 686 (69) |

Rule of thumb: roughly 1,600 conversions per variant to see a 10% lift, 400 for 20%, 75 for 50%. The same arithmetic applies to click-through tests (impressions and clicks). Small accounts should test bold differences, not tweaks.

Hygiene: fix the sample size before launch. Peeking and stopping at the first significant reading turned a nominal 5% false-positive rate into 26.1% in Evan Miller's example [S68]. Run whole weeks, keep one primary metric, check that the lift holds downstream (qualified leads, revenue), and log hypothesis, variants, result and lesson.

## 9. Failures and AI tells

| Tell | Example | Why it fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vague benefit | Boost your productivity | Nothing to picture or check [S1][S2] | Task, time, number |
| Puffery | elevate, unlock, seamless, revolutionize, game-changer, cutting-edge, supercharge, empower | Hype is discounted [S1]; objective text tested 27% better [S27]; on Dry's cliche list [S15] | The fact instead |
| AI vocabulary | delve, tapestry, testament, vibrant, pivotal, crucial, foster, enhance, showcase, underscore | Listed as LLM-overused [S70]; an excess-word study estimated at least 13.5% of 2024 PubMed abstracts were LLM-processed [S71] | Everyday words |
| Copula dodging | serves as, stands as, boasts, offers (for is or has) | A listed LLM pattern [S70] | Use is and has |
| Negative parallelism | It's not just X, it's Y | A listed LLM pattern [S70]; argues with nobody | Say Y |
| Rule of three | Fast, simple and powerful | A listed LLM pattern [S70]; three vague words weigh less than one fact | One specific |
| Dash-heavy rhythm | Em dashes in every sentence | Listed as an AI sign, less common in the newest models [S70]; banned by house style | Comma, colon, full stop |
| Formatting tells | Emoji bullets, bold everywhere, Title Case | Listed [S70] | Plain lines |
| Fake urgency or scarcity | A resetting timer; "only 2 left" when false | Deceptive [S59][S63]; must be genuine [S8] | A real date or stock count |
| AI as the benefit | AI-powered invoicing | Naming artificial intelligence lowered purchase intention, most for risky products [S72] | Lead with the outcome |
| Unscoped superlative | The best, number one, world-class | Needs substantiation [S58][S63] | Scope and source, or cut |
| Template openers | In today's fast-paced world; Are you tired of; Imagine a world; Whether you're X or Y | Burns the fold on nothing | Start at the reader's moment |

How good copywriters avoid them: research first so real specifics exist [S10]; write to one reader [S16]; lead with facts, not adjectives [S17]; cut hard and read aloud [S15]; run the swap test, where a line that still works with a competitor's name gets rewritten (heuristic).

- Strong: Roasted Tuesday, at your door Thursday. This week's Ethiopia tastes like blueberry jam, honestly. Kenya or Colombia next? You pick. #specialtycoffee #coffeeroaster

**X post, product update**
- Weak: Excited to announce our game-changing new feature that will transform your workflow!
- Strong: New in Tallybook: upload a bank statement PDF and get every transaction sorted into tax categories in about 20 seconds. Free on every plan.

## 11. Implications for our skill

### 11.1 Rules

1. No draft without a brief: product, reader, job, awareness level, sophistication guess, offer, placement, the one action, a claim ledger and VOC phrases (or an explicit "no research" flag that marks the output as a draft).
2. When VOC is missing, run a research pass first: mine reviews, Reddit and G2-type sites for verbatim phrases.
3. Angles before words: produce 3 to 5 angles that differ in pain, desire or mechanism, not synonyms of one angle.
4. The first line is the headline and must sell alone inside the fold of its placement.
5. Every factual claim maps to a ledger row. Never invent numbers, reviews, customers, awards, certifications, stock levels or deadlines.
6. One reader, one idea, one action per piece.
7. Plain words: consumer copy at grade 5 to 7 [S29]; B2B may use its readers' trade terms, in short sentences.
8. Persuasion levers only on true facts; urgency only with a real date or stock count.
9. Platform limits and disclosures are hard gates.
10. House style: no em dash, no spaced en dash, no AI tells, the audience's natural voice.

### 11.2 Output templates

- **Meta pack:** 3 angles, each with primary text (the sale complete in the first 125 characters), a Feed headline up to 27 (40 elsewhere), a description up to 30 and a CTA button.
- **Google RSA:** 15 headlines (3 keyword or intent, 3 benefit, 3 proof, 2 offer, 2 objection, 2 CTA), 4 descriptions, 2 paths; every headline must read well beside any other; pin only when required. Performance Max adds long headlines and one headline of 15 characters or fewer.
- **Short video (TikTok, Reels, Shorts):** a 0 to 3 second hook as on-screen text plus voice; product on screen by second 3 to 6; proof or demo; CTA card.
- **Landing page:** H1 (what, for whom, outcome), subhead (how, why believable), proof bar, 3 to 6 feature blocks (value header plus objection paragraph), objection FAQ, risk reversal, one CTA repeated.
- **Organic post:** hook inside the fold, one idea, one ask (a question or a CTA), paid-partnership disclosure first.

### 11.3 Automated checks

- Length per field against section 6, plus a fold check: the core promise sits inside the first 125 characters on Meta and Instagram and the first 140 on LinkedIn.
- Lexicon: the tells in section 9 plus the house list; at most one exclamation mark; emoji and ALL CAPS counts.
- Punctuation: U+2014 anywhere and U+2013 with spaces fail.
- Triplet detector for lists of three adjectives or phrases.
- Claim detector: digits, percentages, superlatives, comparatives, free, guarantee, health, money and green terms, testimonials. Each needs a ledger row or the draft fails.
- Urgency detector: only, last chance, ends, left, timers. Each needs a date or stock source.
- Readability grade and average sentence length.
- CTA check: exactly one primary action, a verb plus the value.
- Platform rules: Instagram hashtags 5 or fewer (3 to 5 specific ones); non-Spark TikTok text without hashtags, @, links or emoji; disclosure present whenever there is a material connection.

### 11.4 Judge rubric (score 1 to 5; ship only when every line scores 4 or more and compliance passes)

1. Clarity: after five seconds a stranger can say what it is, who it is for and what to do.
2. Awareness fit: the first line matches the stated level.
3. Specificity: can the reader picture it, and could someone check it?
4. Distinctiveness: fails if a competitor's name could replace ours.
5. Customer voice: at least one real phrase or situation from VOC.
6. Proof: the key claim has proof beside it, and results shown are typical or qualified.
7. Credibility: no hype and no AI tells; it reads like one person talking to one person.
8. Platform fit: inside limits, native to the format, the fold does the work.
9. Action: one next step that names the value.
10. Compliance and ethics: no fake urgency, unsupported claims, hidden incentives or unproven green claims; disclosures in place.

The judge sees only the brief, the claim ledger, the placement and the copy, never the writer's reasoning, and returns scores, the weakest line and one fix.

### 11.5 Test plan with every paid deliverable

Hypothesis (angle A against angle B), what varies, the primary metric, the sample needed from the table in section 8, and a stop rule written before launch.
