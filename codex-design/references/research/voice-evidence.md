# Evidence and public opinion: how audiences notice AI-written copy, and what reads human (2023 to 2026)

Research pass for the codex-design copy skill. Every source below was read on 2026-09-24.

Scope: consumer studies and surveys, the measured linguistics of model writing, public and practitioner opinion
(with Bangladesh and South Asia), official platform signals, and the ethics line. This file only adds to
`references/copy.md` (sections 1, 2, 5 to 7) and `references/research/copy-ai-tells.md`. Items those files already
cover are marked "(known)" and are not argued again.

## 0. What is new here, in eight lines

1. Readers punish copy they *suspect* is AI, whatever its real origin: trust roughly halves and purchase consideration
   drops 14% (Raptive). Blind, many readers prefer the AI text. Recognition, not quality, triggers the penalty.
2. The penalty is largest on emotional and relational messages (thanks, apologies, replies) and on high-risk products.
3. LinkedIn now names "it's not X, it's Y" as a pattern it demotes (May 2026): the loudest tell is now a platform rule.
4. Measured thresholds (French speeches): ChatGPT's sentence-length CV was 0.50 against 0.78 for the human authors,
   and its 80% spread ratio 1.86 against 3.84.
5. Cliché density is the stylometric feature most tied to AI detection (r = 0.60). Expert editors mostly fix awkward
   wording, weak sentence structure, needless exposition and clichés.
6. A September 2026 study of B2B blog posts: structure alone flags AI copy at 98 F1, even after rewording. The AI
   shape is the tidy, self-announcing post.
7. Bangladesh: AI-made "ordinary people" (police officers, Hindu women, children, shopkeepers) flooded the 2026 election
   campaign, mostly unlabelled. Fake vox pops and testimonials now carry extra risk with Bangladeshi audiences.
8. Law and platforms agree on one line: write naturally, but never invent customers, reviews, quotes or people, and
   disclose where the law or the platform requires it.

## 1. Studies and surveys: trust, engagement, purchase intent

### 1.1 The suspicion penalty

| Source | Sample | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Raptive, July 2025 [S3] | 3,000 US adults; the same articles labelled "AI" or "human" | Believed-AI content scored far worse whatever its real origin: trust down 48%, authenticity down 57%, emotional connection down 60%. For ads next to it, purchase consideration and willingness to pay a premium each fell 14%. |
| NielsenIQ, 12 Dec 2024 [S1] | 2,000+ viewers, about 150 with EEG | Viewers spotted most AI ads and called them annoying, boring and confusing. Even "high quality" AI ads produced weaker memory activation than traditional ads. A negative halo can reach the brand. |
| Bynder, 3 Apr 2024 [S2] | 2,000 (UK and US), two 300-word articles | Half correctly spotted the AI article. 52% say they become less engaged with copy they suspect is AI. AI-seeming social copy made brands look impersonal (25%), untrustworthy (20%), lazy (20%) and uncreative (19%). |
| Nakano et al., Oct 2025 [S8] | 261 Japanese readers, 990 ratings | Being told a text was AI-assisted lowered trust, caring, competence and likability. Readers' reasons: lower credibility (144 mentions), "mechanical" style (132), laziness (103), wrong for the context (82), insincerity (71). |

### 1.2 The blind-test paradox

- Bynder: when readers did not know which article was which, 56% preferred the ChatGPT one. Only 16 to 24-year-olds
  preferred the human article (55%). [S2]
- Zhang and Gosline (2023) tested ad copy for five products and five campaign messages. Blind, GPT-4's copy got the
  highest satisfaction. When authorship was revealed, "written by a human expert" raised ratings, while "AI involved"
  did not lower them. They call it human favoritism, not AI aversion. [S7]
- Porter and Machery (2024): non-experts spotted AI poems below chance (46.6%). They rated the AI poems higher for
  rhythm and beauty and read human complexity as AI incoherence. [S13]
- Chakrabarty, Ginsburg and Dhillon (2025, revised 2026): expert (MFA-trained) readers strongly disliked prompted AI
  prose (quality odds ratio 0.13), while general readers preferred it (1.82). Cliché density was the style feature
  most tied to detector scores (r = 0.60) and carried 16% of the experts' penalty. [S14]
- Russell et al. (ACL 2025): people who use LLMs heavily detect AI articles almost perfectly. Five such experts, by
  majority vote, missed 1 of 300, even after paraphrasing and humanizing. [L9] (known in part)

Reading: fluent AI copy is not punished for being bad. It is punished once the reader recognizes it. Social audiences
now include many heavy users, who recognize it by the tells in section 2.

### 1.3 Where the penalty bites hardest

- **Emotional messages.** Kirk and Givi (Journal of Business Research 186, 2025) ran seven preregistered experiments.
  Believing an emotional marketing message was AI-written lowered positive word of mouth and loyalty. Lower perceived
  authenticity and moral disgust explained the drop. The effect was weaker for factual messages and when AI only edited
  a human draft, and it reversed for copied text (humans who copy are punished more). Kirk's advice, via phys.org:
  "authenticity is always best". [S4]
- **Messages addressed to a person.** In Nakano et al., "Interact" texts lost the most after disclosure. [S8]
  Bynder found over 30% saw chatbot-run brands as impersonal. [S2]
- **The word "AI" itself.** Cicek et al. (WSU, July 2024; 1,000+ US adults, eight product and service categories)
  found that adding "artificial intelligence" to an otherwise identical product description lowered emotional trust
  and purchase intent. The drop was stronger for high-risk products (expensive electronics, medical devices, financial
  services). Cicek: "Focus on describing the features or benefits and avoid the AI buzzwords." [S5]
- **Polished but empty work.** BetterUp with the Stanford Social Media Lab (Sept 2025; 1,150 US desk workers): 40%
  had received AI "workslop" (content that looks good but lacks substance) in the past month, and each case took
  about two hours to handle. [S16]

### 1.4 The disclosure dilemma

- Schilke and Reimann (Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes 188, 2025; 13 experiments): people who
  disclose AI use are trusted less, because the use looks less legitimate. This held across framings and whether
  disclosure was voluntary or mandatory. Being exposed by a third party was worse than disclosing. The penalty
  was smaller, but still there, among evaluators who like technology. [S6]
- IAB 2026: 73% of Gen Z and Millennials say knowing an ad used AI would raise or not change their chance of buying.
  Clear disclosure was the third-strongest attention driver, after quality visuals and humour. Fewer than half of
  advertisers always disclose. [S10]
- Reading: disclose where law or platform requires it (section 5). Being caught costs more than telling. Keep a human
  as the author of emotional and relational messages, where the penalty is largest.

### 1.5 Saturation: why readers are primed

- Liang et al. (Patterns, Oct 2025): by late 2024, about 24% of corporate press releases, 18% of financial consumer
  complaints, about 10% of small-firm job postings and 14% of UN press releases were LLM-assisted. These are lower
  bounds. [L5]
- Graphite (Oct 2025; 43,000 articles): AI-generated articles overtook human ones among new English web articles by
  November 2024, but they largely do not appear in Google or ChatGPT answers. [P10]
- Ahrefs (May 2025; 900,000 new pages): 2.5% pure AI, 71.7% mixed, 25.8% purely human. [P11]
- Originality.ai (July 2026; 5,000 posts): its detector classed 81% of sampled long-form LinkedIn posts as likely AI,
  up from about half in late 2024. It is detector-based, so treat it as a trend, not a fact. [P9]

## 2. Linguistics of model writing: measurable differences

### 2.1 Grammar and rhetoric

- Reinhart et al. (PNAS 122(8), 2025) had each model continue the same human texts. Instruction-tuned models write an
  informationally dense, noun-heavy style whatever the genre. [L1] GPT-4o against the human continuation:

  | Feature | GPT-4o rate against human | Effect size |
  |---|---|---|
  | present participial clauses | 5.3x (known) | d 1.38 |
  | "that" clauses as subject | 2.6x | d 0.77 |
  | nominalizations | 2.1x | d 1.23 |
  | phrasal coordination ("X and Y" pairs) | 1.9x | d 0.81 |
  | agentless passive | about 0.5x (known) | |

  - The Llama base models stay close to human rates. Instruction tuning causes the drift, and with it genre
    misalignment.
  - GPT-4o overuses downtoners ("barely", "nearly"); the Llama variants avoid them.
  - A classifier separates any one model from humans with 93 to 98% accuracy.
- The same pattern shows up in French. Labbé, Labbé and Savoy (2024) compared ChatGPT with French presidents' New
  Year addresses. [L6]
  - Overused: present participles (the top category), adjectives (+13%), common nouns (+7%) and coordinating
    conjunctions.
  - Underused: pronouns (22% fewer), adverbs (32% fewer) and subordinating conjunctions.
  - Favourite words: "we", "must", "continue".
  - The participle and noun habit is not an English quirk. But do not port it blindly to Bengali, where conjunctive
    participles are ordinary (inference).

### 2.2 Vocabulary

- Kobak et al. (Science Advances 11(27), 2 July 2025). [L2]
  - In 15 million+ PubMed abstracts, 454 words showed excess use in 2024. 379 of them were style words: 66% verbs and
    14% adjectives. Before LLMs, excess words were content nouns (covid, pandemic).
  - At least 13.5% of 2024 abstracts went through an LLM, and up to 40% in some subgroups.
  - The signal is style verbs and grand adjectives, not topic words.
- Juzek and Ward (arXiv, Dec 2024) found 21 "focal words". [L3]
  - Rise per million words, 2020 to 2024: delves +6,697%, showcasing +1,396%, boasts +918%, underscores +904%,
    intricate +611%.
  - The list also includes delved, delving, comprehending, intricacies, surpassing, garnered, emphasizing,
    advancements, aligns and groundbreaking.
  - Model tests are consistent with learning from human feedback (RLHF) as one cause.
  - A South Asian reader datapoint: 201 participants in India preferred the abstract without focal words when "delve"
    opened it (p = 0.023).
- Liang et al. (ICML 2024) estimated that 6.5 to 16.9% of ML peer-review text was substantially LLM-modified. [L4]
  - "Commendable" rose 9.8x, "meticulous" 34.7x and "intricate" 11.2x.
  - AI use was higher near deadlines and lower in reviews that cite papers ("et al."). Real references travel with
    human writing.
- Reinhart's most overused words. The authors see in them a taste for grand but hollow summing-up sentences. [L1]
  - GPT-4o: camaraderie 162x, tapestry 155x, intricate 119x, underscore 107x, unspoken 102x, amidst 100x,
    palpable 95x, solace 95x, fleeting 84x, unravel 83x.
  - GPT-4o mini: grapple 131x, ignite 122x, vibrant 92x, cacophony 89x.
- **Word lists age and catch humans.** Yakura et al. (2024, revised 2026) found abrupt rises in "delve", "showcase",
  "boast", "intricacies" and "meticulous" in 737,083 hours of unscripted podcast speech. In a 496-person experiment,
  a short chat with a chatbot made people adopt its words. [L17] Russell et al. found vocabulary was the top expert
  clue (53.1% of explanations), but it mattered less for newer models and humanized text. [L9]

### 2.3 Rhythm and burstiness

Labbé et al. (2024) measured sentence lengths in the French presidential corpus. [L6]

| Measure | Presidents | ChatGPT |
|---|---|---|
| most common length (mode) | 13 words | 19 words |
| median | 19.9 | 20.1 |
| mean | 21.0 | 21.7 |
| standard deviation | 16.4 | 10.9 |
| coefficient of variation (SD / mean) | 0.781 | 0.503 |
| middle 80% of lengths | 12 to 58 words (ratio 3.84) | 14 to 41 words (ratio 1.86) |
| shape | right-skewed: mode < median < mean | near-Gaussian: mode, median and mean almost equal |

- ChatGPT wrote fewer very short (under 15 words) and very long (over 39 words) sentences than the presidents, and
  more mid-length ones.
- Muñoz-Ortiz et al. (Artificial Intelligence Review 57, 2024), on news text: human writing has more scattered
  sentence lengths, more varied vocabulary and shorter constituents. [L7]
- Russell et al.: sentence structure came up in 35.9% of expert explanations. Human sentences vary more in length;
  AI repeats "not only... but also" and lists of three. [L9]

### 2.4 Structure and discourse

- **SlopShape** (Madler, arXiv, v2 17 Sept 2026) is the closest study to marketing copy. [L11]
  - Data: 2,250 pre-ChatGPT blog posts from 268 B2B company sites (SaaS, e-commerce, services, fintech), set against
    11,250 AI versions from GPT-5.4, Claude Sonnet 4.6, Gemini 3 Flash, DeepSeek V3.2 and Kimi K2.5.
  - Structure alone separated them at 98.0 macro-F1, and still 98.1 after rewording.
  - The AI shape is the "tidy, self-announcing" post:
    - the payoff is promised in the title;
    - the thesis and section flow are announced before the first section;
    - the voice is an editorial explainer;
    - the close restates the thesis;
    - stakes are escalated;
    - there is an old-versus-new contrast.
  - Human posts were structurally rarer: rarity percentile 0.84 against 0.44 (d = 1.83). The rarest 1% held 149 human
    posts and 4 AI ones.
  - The features that best pick out each source are listed without their direction, so treat them as leads, not
    rules. Human: decision rules, challenging a false default, how outside voices are brought in. Claude: numbered
    procedures. GPT: numeric density, caveats, sourcing of numbers. Kimi: commercial CTA intensity.
- **StoryScope** (Russell et al., Apr 2026; 61,608 stories) found that AI stories over-explain their themes and prefer
  tidy single-track plots. Human stories are more morally ambiguous and more complex in time. AI stories cluster
  together; narrative features alone keep 97% of detection accuracy. [L12]
- **LAMP** (Chakrabarty, Laban and Wu, CHI 2025). [L8]
  - 18 professional writers made 8,035 edits to 1,057 paragraphs from GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet and Llama 3.1 70B.
  - Most common edit categories: awkward word choice and phrasing 28%, poor sentence structure 20%, unnecessary or
    redundant exposition 18%, clichés 17%. The rest: purple prose, lack of specificity, tense inconsistency.
  - Writers mostly replaced (74%) or deleted (18%); they rarely inserted (8%).
  - No model family wrote better than another.
- **What "slop" means to experts.** Shaib et al. (arXiv, Sept 2025) interviewed experts and coded their answers. [L10]
  - Information utility: density, relevance.
  - Information quality: factuality, bias.
  - Style quality: repetition, templatedness, coherence, fluency, verbosity, word complexity, tone.
  - The strongest predictors of a "slop" verdict were relevance, density and tone. Binary verdicts are partly
    subjective.
  - Kommers et al. (Dec 2025) define slop by three properties: superficial competence, asymmetric effort and mass
    producibility. [L13]

### 2.5 Mixed or genre-dependent signals: do not lint these on one piece

- **Lexical diversity.**
  - ChatGPT essays were more diverse than German students' essays (Herbold et al. 2023). [L14]
  - ChatGPT was less diverse than 240 human writers on six dimensions, and the newest models were the least human-like
    (Kendro et al. 2025). [L15]
  - Human news uses more varied vocabulary (Muñoz-Ortiz). [L7]
  - So type-token ratio on short copy is unreliable. Measure repetition across a set of pieces instead.
- **Hedging.**
  - Models rarely express uncertainty: 47% of their confident answers were wrong, and human preference data penalizes
    uncertainty (Zhou et al., ACL 2024). [L16]
  - GPT-4o overuses downtoners (Reinhart). Keep the known "stacked hedge" rule; a single hedge is human.
- **Numbers.** LLM news uses more numbers and symbols (Muñoz-Ortiz), and ChatGPT speeches use more numbers (Labbé).
  A number reads human only when it is real, sourced and specific to this client.
- **Pronouns.** Fewer pronouns but more "we" (Labbé, speeches); more pronouns (Muñoz-Ortiz, news). The direction
  depends on the genre.
- **Grammar.** AI text is near error-free, and human text has more minor errors: 19.6% against 14.8% of profiles in
  Jakesch et al. [S9] But readers wrongly read errors as AI. Never plant errors.
- **Emotion.** Human news carries more fear and disgust and less joy (Muñoz-Ortiz). This supports the
  "relentless positivity" tell below.

### 2.6 What readers actually look at

- **Expert readers** (Russell et al.). How often each clue category came up in expert explanations: [L9]

  | Clue | Share | What the experts saw |
  |---|---|---|
  | vocabulary | 53.1% | AI words |
  | sentence structure | 35.9% | predictable patterns, even lengths |
  | grammar and punctuation | 24.8% | AI text is grammatically perfect; human text mixes brackets, quotes and bursts of commas |
  | originality | 23.7% | AI is safe, without surprise or humour |
  | quotes | 22.3% | AI quotes sound formal and speak in the same voice as the article |
  | clarity | 19.5% | over-explaining, telling instead of showing |

  - AI names: "Emily" or "Sarah" appeared in 63.3% of GPT-4o articles and 70% of Claude 3.5 Sonnet articles.
  - Humanized AI text leaned on titles ("Dr.", "Prof.") for the people it quoted.
- **Lay readers** (Jakesch, Hancock and Naaman, PNAS 120(11), 2023; six experiments, 4,600 participants). [S9]
  - People spotted AI self-descriptions at only 50 to 52% accuracy.
  - The reasons they gave:
    - content (40%): family and life experience read as human;
    - grammar (28%): first person and polish read as human;
    - tone (24%): warm and genuine read as human, impersonal and monotonous as AI.
  - Valid cues: nonsensical content (13.6% of AI texts against 9.6% of human texts) and repetitive content (12.7%
    against 7.1%). A repetitive text was 47% more likely to be AI.
  - Flawed cues: errors, long words, first person and family topics.
  - The authors showed that models can exploit the flawed cues. That is exactly why the skill must not fake them.

## 3. Public opinion and community voices

### 3.1 "Slop" became the public word

- "AI slop" was Macquarie Dictionary's Word of the Year 2025 (24 Nov 2025). The committee and the public agreed,
  only the fourth time in its history. [P2]
- "Slop" was Merriam-Webster's 2025 word of the year (15 Dec 2025). Its sense: low-quality digital content that AI
  produces in quantity. [P1]
- Wikipedia's "AI slop" article records that the American Dialect Society also chose "slop" for 2025. [P3]
- Kapwing (28 Nov 2025), on a fresh YouTube account: of the first 500 Shorts, 21% were AI slop and 33% "brainrot". [P4]
  - South Asia: the Indian channel "Bandar Apna Dost" had the most views of any AI slop channel (2.07 billion).
  - Pakistan had 20 AI slop channels in its top 100, the most of any country studied.
- Backlash to AI-made brand work, as recorded on Wikipedia: [P3]
  - Coca-Cola's AI holiday ads (2024, repeated in 2025).
  - McDonald's Netherlands pulled its AI Christmas ad (Dec 2025).
  - Paramount's "Novocaine" Instagram promo drew criticism for an AI script and robotic narration (Mar 2025).
  - Svedka's AI Super Bowl ad (2026) was heavily criticized.

### 3.2 What practitioners and communities say (practitioner consensus and anecdote)

- Sean Kernan, a full-time writer and editor (21 Apr 2025), lists 13 signs. [P5]
  - The signs: rigid, repetitive phrasing; generic content; only second and third person; jumping straight into
    lists; no specifics or sensory detail; em dashes; "it's not X, it's Y"; no typos; long, same-shaped sentences;
    inconsistency between posts; heavy hedging; colon titles; blogging clichés.
  - He says readers resent AI writing they were not told about.
- Vertical Rail, an agency blog by Megan Anfinson (dated "August 24", year not shown) [P6]:
  - Audiences notice the suspiciously even rhythm, the em dash, and captions about "authentic connection" that sound
    factory-made.
  - Its advice: let AI take a draft to about 60%, then have a human set the voice, and spend the saved time on real
    replies in the comments.
- Reddit, through reporting.
  - r/changemyview users uncovered the University of Zurich's undisclosed AI persona experiment (Apr 2025). [P7]
  - Reddit's July 2026 crackdown targets brands that plant AI-written "honest opinion" posts so chatbots will cite
    them. Its systems caught about 25,000 spammy posts and comments a day in Q1 2026 and cut exposure 20% year on
    year. [P8]
  - Reddit says it looks for coordinated fake behaviour and artificial hype, and checks signals when an account is
    created.
- LinkedIn members now have a "Seems like AI slop" report button (July 2026). [PL8]

### 3.3 Bangladesh and South Asia

Known from copy.md §3: Prothom Alo, Niropekho and Bigganchinta (2025) on AI Bengali and the misused em dash.

New voices:
- **The Daily Star, 21 Feb 2026, "Bangla in the age of algorithms".** [B1]
  - Dr Tariq Manzoor, Professor of Bangla at Dhaka University: "AI's language is mechanical". He says teachers
    notice it in assignments and creative writing.
  - Nishat Raihan, an LLM researcher at George Mason University: LLMs work within about 10 to 20 response patterns,
    which narrows how young people write.
  - For Bengali copy, sameness across pieces is a real risk.
- **BBC News Bangla, 27 July 2025.** [B2]
  - AI campaign videos staged fake ordinary people asking for votes, among them a sindoor-wearing woman and a rickshaw
    puller.
  - Dismislab analysed 70 such videos with over 23 million views and over a million reactions. Most run in
    8-second cuts, a trace of Google's Veo tool.
  - An adviser to the government shared AI clips before realising what they were.
  - Dhaka University's Dr Saiful Alam Chowdhury: people seize on fakes that suit their side.
- **Dismislab, 31 Jan 2026.** [B3]
  - Over 800 AI videos from 21 pages in the first half of January 2026: fake police officers, Hindu women,
    children, shopkeepers and "commoners" endorsing parties.
  - Unlabelled: 60% on Facebook, 94.48% of 181 YouTube videos, and all 50 TikTok videos. Facebook's "AI info" label did
    not show on Facebook Lite or the web.
  - Commenters treated the characters as real.
  - ULAB's Sumon Rahman pointed to low AI literacy and election-time confirmation bias.
  - The Election Commission voiced concern on 12 Dec 2025 and 20 Jan 2026.
- **Dismislab, 28 Feb 2026.** Nine fact-checkers debunked 1,185 political misinformation items between 11 Dec 2025
  and 20 Feb 2026. About 12% of the election-related ones were AI-made (150 AI cases in all), about 70% of them video,
  and much of it was styled to look like news. [B4]
- The Election Commission reportedly detected about 86,000 AI-generated disinformation items before the February 2026
  election. This comes from Bangladesh Pratidin (6 Feb 2026) as cited on Wikipedia; it was not read directly. [B5]
- **The Daily Star, 13 Sept 2026.** Sajid Mahbub of Bangladesh Brand Forum: generative AI moves the scarce skill from
  production to judgment, and over-targeted personalisation can hurt trust. [B6]

Reading for Bangladeshi copy:
- Bangladeshi audiences now meet AI-made "real people" as a disinformation pattern. Invented vox pops, fake customers
  or staged testimonials are an ethical breach, and a fast way to lose trust.
- Mechanical, templated Bangla is noticed. The existing §3 rules stay central.

## 5. Ethics: writing naturally versus deceiving people

### 5.1 Where the line sits

Legitimate:
- Writing in the audience's own register.
- Removing tells.
- Drafting with AI and editing as a human.
- Using the client's real facts, people and voice.

Deceptive:
- Presenting an invented person, experience or opinion as real.
- Hiding AI use where law or platform requires disclosure.
- Faking organic activity.

### 5.2 Rules that must stay in the skill

1. **Never invent customers, reviews, testimonials, quotes, ratings or follower counts.**
   - The FTC's final rule (announced 14 Aug 2024, in force since October 2024) bans fake reviews and testimonials. That
     explicitly includes AI-generated ones attributed to people who do not exist or who have no real experience of the
     product. It also bans conditioned incentives, undisclosed insider reviews, fake "independent" review sites, review
     suppression and fake social indicators. [E1]
   - The FTC's Endorsement Guides: an endorsement must reflect the honest opinion of someone who really used the
     product. [E2]
   - UK: under the DMCC Act, in force since April 2025, a fake review is one that claims to be, but is not, based on
     genuine experience. The CMA's guidance (4 Apr 2025) counts buying bot-generated reviews as a banned practice. The
     ban on commissioning covers influencers and marketing firms, and incentivised reviews must be clearly labelled.
     [E3]
2. **No AI personas presented as real people:** no fake staff, fake experts, fake vox pops or "ordinary customers".
   - The CMA guidance also points to the banned practice of falsely representing oneself as a consumer. [E3]
   - The University of Zurich placed 1,700+ AI comments on r/changemyview through personas such as a trauma survivor,
     tailored with inferred personal details. The result was community outrage and formal legal demands from Reddit.
     [P7]
   - Bangladesh's 2026 election showed the same pattern at scale. [B3]
3. **Disclose where the law or the platform requires it.**
   - Label realistic synthetic images, video and audio on Meta, TikTok and YouTube. [PL2][PL6][B3]
   - EU AI Act Article 50 applies from 2 Aug 2026. [E4]
     - A chatbot must say it is AI unless that is obvious.
     - Deepfakes must be disclosed.
     - AI-generated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest must be disclosed, unless a
       human reviewed it and holds editorial responsibility.
     - Providers must mark synthetic output in a machine-readable way. The implementation timeline gives systems
       already on the market a short grace period for that.
4. **No astroturfing or seeding.**
   - No planted "honest opinions" for search or chatbot visibility. [P8]
   - No automated comment tools: LinkedIn says they breach its terms. [PL7]
5. **Real specifics only.** Never invent a number, a name, a story or a family detail to seem human. Jakesch et al. show
   that fake human cues work on readers, which is why using them is manipulation. [S9]
6. **Natural register, not fake imperfection.** Contractions, fragments and the market's slang are fine. Planted typos
   and staged "rawness" are not; Mosseri notes that AI will copy the raw look anyway. [PL5]
7. **Write for readers, not detectors.** The goal is copy that is true, specific and native to the audience.
   Humanizing text to dodge a disclosure duty is out of scope. Expert readers see through humanizers anyway. [L9]
   (known)
8. **Keep a human owner.** The EU exception rests on human editorial responsibility. Kirk and Givi found the penalty
   shrinks when AI only edits. Emotional or relational messages should be written, or at least rewritten, by the
   person who signs them. [E4][S4]

## 6. Prioritised list: the twenty features that most make copy feel machine-made or human

Ranked by the strength and breadth of the evidence that readers notice the feature and that it costs trust or reach,
weighted by how common it is in marketing and social copy.

Evidence strength:
- **Study**: peer-reviewed or preprint data. "Strong" means several independent studies.
- **Survey**: a consumer or industry survey.
- **Platform**: an official platform statement or policy.
- **Consensus**: several practitioner sources agree.
- **Anecdote**: a single case.

## 7. Measurable signals for a linter

"Known" means the rule already exists in `copy-ai-tells.md` §F or `copylint`; the new evidence only confirms it. The
thresholds from studies come from speeches, news and essays; calibrate them on short social copy against the client's
own human posts and the 12 Bangladeshi brand pages already sampled for copy.md §3.

| # | Signal | How to measure | Threshold | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sentence-length CV (8+ sentences) | SD / mean of words per sentence | Warn under 0.55; strong under 0.50 | L6: ChatGPT 0.503, humans 0.781. Known threshold (clay 0.55) confirmed |
| 2 | Length spread (20+ sentences) | 90th percentile length / 10th percentile length | Warn under 2.0 | L6: 1.86 against 3.84 |
| 3 | Missing extremes (10+ sentences) | Count sentences of 6 words or fewer and of 25 words or more | Note when there are none of either | L6 (ChatGPT writes fewer of both; cut-offs adapted to ad copy, an inference) |
| 4 | Gaussian shape (20+ sentences) | Mode, median and mean of lengths | Note when all three sit within 1 word of each other | L6 (humans: mode < median < mean) |

| 6 | Trailing "-ing" riders | Share of sentences ending ", [verb]ing ..." | Warn at 0.15 or more | Known (clay); L1 (5.3x) |

| 10 | Restated-thesis closer | Last paragraph opens with `(in short|in summary|to sum up|in conclusion|ultimately|the bottom line|at the end of the day)` | Warn | L11 |
| 11 | Old-against-new frame | `\b(gone are the days|the old way|traditional(ly)?\b[^.]{0,40}\b(now|today)\b)` | Note | L11 |
| 12 | Stakes escalation | `\b(costing you|every day you wait|before it'?s too late|make or break|the difference between)\b` | Note | L11 (inference for the phrase list) |

| 14 | Unsourced quote or testimonial | Quoted speech, star rating or "customer" line not present in the brief | Error | E1, E3 (ethics) |

Not worth linting per piece (section 2.5): type-token ratio, single hedges, pronoun share on its own, error counts.

## 8. What this suggests for the skill (short)

1. Add the SlopShape structure checks (rows 9 to 12) and the cross-piece sameness check (row 18). Structure survives
   rewording; word lists do not.
2. Add a cliché-density measure (row 5). It is the best-supported single style signal after the contrast frame.
3. Tighten the testimonial rule into an error (row 14) and name the Emily and Sarah default (row 13).
4. For relational copy (replies, apologies, thanks, condolences, festival greetings) require a human-owned rewrite.
   The evidence says that is where readers punish AI most.
5. In Bangladeshi work, treat any "ordinary person" voice (vox pop, customer, rickshaw puller, homemaker) as real-only.
   The 2026 election made staged AI people a recognised deception pattern.
6. In copyjudge, weight "specific to this brand" and "has a point of view" heavily. Platforms and readers both reward
   them, and they are what blind-preferred AI copy lacks once it is recognised.
